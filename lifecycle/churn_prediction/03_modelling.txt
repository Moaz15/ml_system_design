CASE 01 - MODELLING
===================
Read 02_label.txt first. Everything here assumes the (user, cycle) row,
the T-7 snapshot, and the assigned_arm column.


#######################################################################
1. PROPENSITY VERSUS UPLIFT, SIDE BY SIDE
#######################################################################

                      PROPENSITY              UPLIFT
  -------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question            who will churn?         whose decision changes?
  Target              P(renew | x)            tau(x) = treated minus
                                              control
  Label               observed directly       NEVER observed for any
                                              individual
  Data needed         any history             randomised treat and
                                              control
  Methods             logistic, GBM           T-, S-, X-learner,
                                              causal forest
  Metric              AUC, log-loss,          Qini, uplift curve,
                      calibration             AUUC
  Used for            rank by risk, set a     rank by tau, allocate a
                      threshold               budget
  Blind to            Sure Things and         nothing that matters
                      Sleeping Dogs           here

Three consequences, and the first is the one that gets argued about:

THE FUNDAMENTAL PROBLEM OF CAUSAL INFERENCE. A given user was either
treated or not. You never observe both outcomes for the same person, so
tau(x) has no per-user ground truth, ever. It is estimated across
groups and validated only in aggregate. Anyone who asks to see the
model's accuracy on individual uplift predictions is asking for
something that does not exist.

AUC IS UNUSABLE ON UPLIFT. There is no true per-user value to score
against. Reporting AUC for an uplift model means you have quietly
scored the propensity component instead and called it the uplift model.

ESTIMATING A DIFFERENCE IS EXPENSIVE. The variance of a difference is
the sum of the variances. This is why the sample arithmetic in
02_label.txt section 7 constrains the roadmap more than any engineering
work does.

A terminology trap worth clearing up before someone else does:
PROPENSITY TO CHURN (a predictive score, what growth means) is not a
PROPENSITY SCORE (P(treat | x), used to adjust for confounding). They
appear in the same sentence in this project and they are unrelated.


#######################################################################
2. THREE MODELS, AND WHAT EACH ONE IS FOR
#######################################################################

  M1  PROPENSITY        P(renew | x)       calibrated GBM.
                                           ELIGIBILITY AND MONITORING
                                           ONLY. It does not do the
                                           targeting
  M2  UPLIFT / CATE     tau(x, arm)        T- or X-learner, or a
                                           causal forest. Fit ONLY on
                                           randomised rows
  M3  PRICE-RESPONSE    tau(x, d)          the dose-response curve
                                           across depths, with a
                                           monotonicity constraint

M1 being demoted to eligibility is a deliberate, defensible choice and
it should be said rather than implied. The propensity model still earns
its place: it supplies the baseline probability that sets the price
term in the optimiser, it filters out people who are not in a decision
at all, and it is the series that tells you whether the population is
drifting. What it does not do is choose who gets money.


#######################################################################
3. STEP BY STEP
#######################################################################

STEP 1 - BASELINE RENEWAL, mu_0(x)
----------------------------------
Train on CONTROL ROWS ONLY: renewed regressed on features.

Gradient-boosted trees, then calibrated with isotonic regression or
Platt scaling on a held-out slice. Output: P(renew | x, no offer).

This is the churn model everyone asked for, and it is step one of four.
Calibration is not optional here and the reason is specific: mu_0 is
multiplied by a price to produce an expected revenue, so a probability
that is merely well-RANKED is useless. A model that says 0.8 when the
truth is 0.5 will mis-rank the entire budget allocation while posting
an excellent AUC.

STEP 2 - TREATED RENEWAL, mu_1(x, d)
------------------------------------
Train on TREATED rows. Two ways to do it:

  - one model per depth (the T-learner): simple, honest, and high
    variance when a depth arm is thin
  - one model with d as a feature, under a MONOTONICITY CONSTRAINT so
    that a deeper discount can never predict a worse renewal
    probability. LightGBM supports this directly

The monotonicity constraint is doing real work. Without it, a thin d30
arm will produce pockets of the feature space where 30% off predicts
lower renewal than 20% off, the optimiser will exploit exactly those
pockets, and the resulting policy will be noise dressed up as a
decision.

STEP 3 - UPLIFT
---------------
  tau(x, d) = mu_1(x, d) - mu_0(x)

That is the T-learner: two models, subtract. It is the version to
describe first because it is transparent.

The X-learner improves on it and is worth knowing by name and by
mechanism: impute each unit's individual effect using the OTHER arm's
model, regress those imputed effects on features, and combine the two
resulting estimators weighted by the propensity score. It is better
precisely when the arms are imbalanced, which they always are here -
control is deliberately small, because control costs retained revenue.

A causal forest is the alternative, and splits on treatment effect
heterogeneity directly rather than on outcome. Worth reaching for when
the heterogeneity is the product, which here it is. Start at the
T-learner, move when the Qini curve says the extra machinery earns
something.

STEP 4 - THE POLICY
-------------------
For each user, for each depth d in the grid:

    expected_revenue(d) = (mu_0 + tau(x,d)) * plan_price * (1 - d)
    gain(d)             = expected_revenue(d) - expected_revenue(0)
    cost(d)             = (mu_0 + tau(x,d)) * plan_price * d

Choose d* = argmax over d of gain(d). Then rank all users by
gain / cost and treat down that list until the margin budget is spent.

Ranking by gain/cost rather than by gain is what makes it a budget
allocation rather than a wish list - it is a greedy knapsack, and with
a Lagrangian on the budget constraint it is the same thing done
properly. Users with a negative best gain are not merely skipped, they
are SUPPRESSED, which is the Sleeping Dog guard falling out of the
arithmetic rather than being bolted on as a rule.


#######################################################################
4. WHY THE DEPTH GRID IS DISCRETE
#######################################################################

Depth comes from a small grid - 0 / 10 / 20 / 30 percent - and not from
a continuous optimisation.

The reason is identification, not simplicity. Randomising over four
arms gives four estimable cells. Randomising over a continuous depth
gives a dose-response surface fit from data that is thin everywhere and
is being extrapolated at exactly the depths the optimiser will choose.
Four arms also keep the experiment explainable to the people who have
to approve it, and keep every reported number traceable to a cell
somebody can count rows in.


#######################################################################
5. WORKED EXAMPLE - TWO USERS, ONE MODEL
#######################################################################

USER A - SURE THING. Heavy listener, autopay on, nine cycles of
tenure. mu_0 = 0.62, plan price 299.

  DEPTH   tau     P(renew)  PRICE PAID  EXP REVENUE  GAIN
  -------------------------------------------------------------------
  0%      -       0.62      299         185.4        -
  10%     +0.03   0.65      269         174.9        -10.5
  20%     +0.05   0.67      239         160.2        -25.2
  30%     +0.06   0.68      209         142.3        -43.1

Every depth loses money. Note what happened: the discount DID raise
renewal probability at every depth - the offer works - and it still
destroys value, because the probability gain is smaller than the price
given up. A churn model would have skipped User A too, but only by
luck, because it scored them safe. The uplift model skips them for the
right reason: tau is small because they renew either way.

USER B - PERSUADABLE. Usage halved since last cycle, autopay off, took
a discount once before. mu_0 = 0.30, plan price 299.

  DEPTH   tau     P(renew)  PRICE PAID  EXP REVENUE  GAIN
  -------------------------------------------------------------------
  0%      -       0.30      299         89.7         -
  10%     +0.12   0.42      269         113.0        +23.3
  20%     +0.25   0.55      239         131.6        +41.9
  30%     +0.30   0.60      209         125.5        +35.8

d* = 20%. Thirty percent off CONVERTS MORE PEOPLE AND EARNS LESS, and
that single line is the entire argument for a dose-response curve
rather than one fixed discount depth. A system that optimises renewal
rate picks 30% here and is wrong by Rs 6 per user, every cycle, across
the whole treated population.

  cost(20%) = 0.55 * 299 * 0.20 = 32.9
  gain/cost = 41.9 / 32.9 = 1.27

User B enters the budget ranking at 1.27. User A never enters it at
all.


#######################################################################
6. WHAT THE SYSTEM ACTUALLY OUTPUTS
#######################################################################

  user    cycle  mu_0  best_d  gain  gain/cost  DECISION
  -------------------------------------------------------------------
  u_102   c_02   0.30  20%     41.9  1.27       treat at 20%
  u_109   c_04   0.41  10%     18.2  0.94       treat at 10%
  u_101   c_07   0.62  0%      -     -          skip (Sure Thing)
  u_117   c_01   0.08  30%     2.1   0.09       skip (Lost Cause)
  u_133   c_09   0.55  0%      -6.4  -          SUPPRESS (negative
                                                tau - Sleeping Dog)
  u_140   c_06   0.34  -       -     -          holdout, no contact

Ranked by gain/cost, cut where the budget runs out.

Note the shape of that table, because it is the finding that surprises
stakeholders: MOST USERS GET NOTHING. A good uplift policy treats a
minority, and the minority it treats is not the highest-risk minority.
Note too that u_117, the user most likely to churn in the whole table,
is skipped. Explaining why that is correct is the moment the project
either gets approved or does not.
