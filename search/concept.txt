SEARCH - THE CONCEPTS
=====================
IF YOU REMEMBER ONLY FIVE THINGS

  1. Search is a funnel because you cannot score ten million documents
     with an expensive model in 200 milliseconds. Every architectural
     choice in search is a consequence of that sentence.

  2. Retrieval optimises RECALL. Ranking optimises ORDER. A document
     that retrieval fails to return can never be rescued by any ranker,
     however good. Losses at the top of the funnel are permanent.

  3. Lexical and semantic retrieval fail in OPPOSITE directions, which
     is why production systems run both and fuse them.

  4. Clicks are not relevance. Users click the top result because it is
     on top. Train on raw clicks and the model learns to reproduce
     whatever was already ranked first.

  5. The query type decides everything. "mirzapur" and "something scary
     to listen to tonight" are not the same problem and must not be
     served by the same logic or judged by the same metric.


#######################################################################
PART 1 - THE ONE CONSTRAINT, AND WHAT IT FORCES
#######################################################################

You have, say, 10,000,000 documents and about 200ms of budget.

A model good enough to judge relevance properly - one that reads the
query and the document together - takes maybe 20ms per document. Ten
million of those is 55 hours. So you cannot do the good thing to
everything.

The resolution is a funnel: do something cheap to everything, then
something expensive to the survivors.

    query
      |
      v
    QUERY UNDERSTANDING          cheap, once per query
      |                          normalise, correct, classify, expand
      v
    RETRIEVAL                    cheap per doc, runs over MILLIONS
      |                          returns ~500-1000 candidates
      |                          goal: do not lose the right answer
      v
    RANKING                      expensive per doc, runs over ~1000
      |                          returns ~50 ordered
      |                          goal: get the order right
      v
    RE-RANKING / POLICY          very expensive or rule-based, over ~50
      |                          diversity, freshness, business rules,
      |                          dedup, blending
      v
    results

Each stage is ~1000x smaller than the one before, so each stage can
afford a model ~1000x more expensive. That is the whole trick.

THE ASYMMETRY THAT MATTERS MOST
Retrieval and ranking are not symmetric in their failure. If ranking
puts the right answer at position 4 instead of 1, you lose some value.
If RETRIEVAL never returns it, you lose all of it, and no downstream
component can tell that anything went wrong.

This is why retrieval is measured separately, on its own metric -
recall@1000, the share of known-relevant documents that survive into the
candidate set. A system with NDCG problems may have a ranking bug. A
system with a recall@1000 of 0.6 has a ceiling it can never exceed, and
tuning the ranker is wasted effort until that is fixed.


#######################################################################
PART 2 - THE INVERTED INDEX
#######################################################################

The reason search is fast at all. Instead of scanning documents for the
query, you precompute the reverse map: term -> the documents containing
it.

    "mirzapur"  ->  [doc 5, doc 92, doc 4001, ...]
    "thriller"  ->  [doc 5, doc 17, doc 88,  ...]

A query for "mirzapur thriller" fetches two POSTING LISTS and combines
them. No document is ever read. It is a dictionary lookup, not magic.

ANALYSIS is what turns raw text into the terms that go in that
dictionary, at index time and again at query time:

  - tokenisation      split text into terms; hard for languages without
                      spaces, and for code-mixed text
  - lowercasing and unicode normalisation
  - stemming / lemmatisation   "running" -> "run"; crude but cheap
  - stop words        drop "the", "of"; less common now, it loses
                      phrases like "the who"
  - synonyms          expand at index time or at query time, not both

THE CLASSIC BUG, worth knowing because it is a "have you shipped this"
signal: the analyser at INDEX time and the analyser at QUERY time must
be identical. Stem the documents but not the query and nothing matches,
silently, with no error anywhere.


#######################################################################
PART 3 - LEXICAL RETRIEVAL: TF-IDF AND BM25
#######################################################################

The intuition is three observations:

  1. A term appearing often in a document suggests the document is
     about it.                                    -> term frequency, TF
  2. A term appearing in nearly every document tells you nothing.
     "the" is worthless, "mirzapur" is decisive.
                                        -> inverse document frequency
  3. A long document contains more of every word by accident, and must
     not get credit for that.               -> length normalisation

TF-IDF multiplies the first two. BM25 is the same idea with two fixes
that matter in practice:

  - SATURATION. The 20th occurrence of "mirzapur" does not make a
    document 20x more relevant than the 1st. BM25 flattens out.
  - proper length normalisation, tunable.

                       f(q,D) * (k1 + 1)
  score = SUM IDF(q) * ---------------------------------------
          q in Q        f(q,D) + k1 * (1 - b + b * |D|/avgdl)

  k1 (~1.2-2.0) controls how fast TF saturates.
  b  (~0.75)    controls how hard long documents are penalised.

You do not need to recite this. You DO need to know that BM25 is the
default baseline, that it is free, that it needs no training data, and
that it is genuinely hard to beat. In search it plays the role seasonal
naive plays in forecasting: if your neural retriever does not clearly
beat BM25, you do not have a neural retriever, you have a liability.


#######################################################################
PART 4 - WHY LEXICAL IS NOT ENOUGH: VOCABULARY MISMATCH
#######################################################################

The user types "cheap phone". The document says "budget smartphone".
Zero shared terms. BM25 scores it zero. The right answer is invisible,
and it is invisible at the RETRIEVAL stage, so it is gone for good.

That single failure is the reason embeddings exist in search.

But run the argument backwards before you reach for vectors. The user
types "mirzapur". A document is titled exactly "Mirzapur". Lexical
retrieval nails this instantly. A semantic model may helpfully decide
that "Gangs of Wasseypur" is nearby in meaning and rank it up - which
is not helpful at all, it is wrong.

    LEXICAL is strong on   exact terms, rare words, names, IDs, codes,
                           numbers, anything the user typed literally
    LEXICAL is weak on     synonyms, paraphrase, description, intent

    SEMANTIC is strong on  meaning, paraphrase, vague description,
                           cross-lingual matching
    SEMANTIC is weak on    exact match, rare entities it never saw in
                           training, numbers, IDs, negation

Their failure modes are complementary. Hold that thought for Part 6.


#######################################################################
PART 5 - SEMANTIC RETRIEVAL: TWO TOWERS AND ANN
#######################################################################

The idea: map queries and documents into the same vector space, trained
so that "close together" means "relevant to each other". Then retrieval
becomes nearest-neighbour search.

WHY TWO SEPARATE TOWERS, AND NOT ONE MODEL
This is the concept to actually understand, because it is the same
latency-times-cardinality constraint from Part 1 showing up inside the
model architecture.

  BI-ENCODER (two towers)
    Document tower encodes every document OFFLINE, once. Query tower
    encodes the query at request time, once. Relevance is a dot product
    between two vectors that were computed independently.
    -> 10M documents cost nothing at query time. Fast. Less accurate,
       because the model never sees query and document together.

  CROSS-ENCODER
    Query and document are concatenated and read together with full
    attention. Far more accurate - the model can actually compare them.
    -> Must run once per (query, document) PAIR at request time.
       Impossible over 10M documents. Fine over 100.

So: bi-encoder for retrieval, cross-encoder for re-ranking the top
~100. The funnel again, one level down.

ANN - APPROXIMATE NEAREST NEIGHBOUR
Even with precomputed vectors, comparing the query against 10M vectors
exactly is too slow. So you approximate:

  HNSW   a navigable graph; walk toward closer neighbours. Fast, high
         recall, memory hungry. The common default.
  IVF    cluster the vectors, search only the nearest few clusters.
  PQ     compress vectors into small codes; trades accuracy for memory.

"Approximate" is not a figure of speech. You WILL sometimes miss the
true nearest neighbour. That is a recall loss at the retrieval stage -
the permanent kind. The tuning knob trades recall against latency and
memory, and you should know which way you have turned it.

EMBEDDINGS ARE A PIPELINE, NOT A MODEL
Changing the embedding model means re-encoding the entire corpus and
rebuilding the index. Query-side and document-side embeddings must come
from the same model version, or the space is incoherent and results are
nonsense. Plan the migration before you pick the model.


#######################################################################
PART 6 - HYBRID RETRIEVAL AND FUSION
#######################################################################

Since lexical and semantic fail in opposite directions, run both and
merge. Almost every serious production system does this.

The problem in merging is that BM25 scores and cosine similarities are
on incomparable scales, and BM25 scores are not even comparable across
queries. Two answers:

  SCORE FUSION     normalise each system's scores, then weighted sum.
                   Needs tuning, and the normalisation is fragile.

  RECIPROCAL RANK FUSION (RRF)   ignore scores entirely, use RANKS.

                              1
      RRF(d) = SUM  -------------------
               i      k + rank_i(d)          k is typically 60

RRF is almost embarrassingly simple, needs no tuning, no score
calibration, and is very hard to beat. Know it by name; it comes up.


#######################################################################
PART 7 - QUERY UNDERSTANDING
#######################################################################

Everything that happens before retrieval. Cheap, high leverage, and the
part weak candidates skip entirely.

  - normalisation: case, unicode, transliteration (Hindi typed in Latin
    script is one string to the user and a different string to the index)
  - spelling correction, with a "did you mean" fallback
  - entity and attribute extraction:
        "red nike shoes under 3000"
          -> colour=red, brand=nike, category=shoes, price<3000
    Once extracted these become FILTERS, not scored terms, which is a
    completely different retrieval path.
  - query expansion: add synonyms and related terms
  - query rewriting: rewrite to a canonical form
  - intent classification, which is the important one:

THE QUERY TAXONOMY - the single most useful cut in search

  NAVIGATIONAL / KNOWN-ITEM     "mirzapur", "iphone 15 pro"
    The user knows exactly what they want. There is ONE right answer.
    Exact match must win. Failure is catastrophic and extremely visible,
    and it is the classic way an engagement-trained ranker destroys
    value: it deranks the exact match in favour of something more
    clickable. Metric: MRR, or plain accuracy@1.

  BROAD / EXPLORATORY           "thriller audiobooks", "something funny"
    Many right answers. Diversity and coverage matter. Metric: NDCG.

  LONG TAIL                     rare, specific, often zero-result
    Little or no behavioural data exists for these queries, so
    click-trained models are blind here and lexical matching carries
    most of the weight.

HEAD VS TAIL
Query frequency follows a Zipf distribution: a tiny number of distinct
queries make up a huge share of traffic, and an enormous number of
distinct queries each appear once or twice. This splits your strategy:
head queries can be cached, curated, even hand-tuned; tail queries are
where the model has to actually generalise. Reporting one average metric
across both hides everything.


#######################################################################
PART 8 - RANKING
#######################################################################

FEATURE FAMILIES

  query features          length, intent class, language, rarity
  document features       quality, popularity, recency, length,
                          completeness, price, rating
  match features          BM25 score, embedding similarity, field-level
                          matches (title vs body vs tags), proximity
  context features        device, time, location, session history
  behavioural features    historical CTR / conversion for this
                          query-document pair

Behavioural features are the most powerful and the most dangerous. They
encode what previous rankings showed, so they hard-wire the feedback
loop into the model. See Part 9.

LEARNING TO RANK

  POINTWISE   predict a relevance score per document independently.
              Simple. Ignores that ranking is inherently relative.
  PAIRWISE    predict which of two documents should rank higher.
              RankNet, LambdaRank. Closer to the real objective.
  LISTWISE    optimise a list metric such as NDCG directly.
              LambdaMART is the canonical one.

LambdaMART - gradient boosted trees with a listwise objective - remains
a very strong production default. Neural rankers win when you have
enormous behavioural data and rich text; GBDT wins on tabular features
and is far easier to debug. Saying "GBDT unless I have a specific
reason" is a strong answer, not a weak one.

RE-RANKING AND POLICY
The last stage, over ~50 documents, where the things that are not
relevance get applied: diversity (do not return ten near-identical
results), freshness boosts, dedup, business rules, promoted content,
and blending across verticals.


#######################################################################
PART 9 - WHERE RELEVANCE LABELS COME FROM
#######################################################################

Unlike recommendation, search HAS a notion of ground truth, because the
user declared their intent. That makes human judgement possible, which
is a real advantage. Two sources, and they disagree:

HUMAN RELEVANCE JUDGEMENTS
  Trained raters grade (query, document) pairs against a written
  guideline, usually on a graded scale - Perfect / Excellent / Good /
  Fair / Bad. Slow and expensive, roughly a few rupees to a few dollars
  per judgement, but unbiased by position and obtainable for tail
  queries that have no traffic at all.
  What makes or breaks it: the GUIDELINE. Relevance is a policy someone
  has to write down. Measure inter-rater agreement; if raters disagree
  with each other, your labels have a noise floor and no model can beat
  it.

BEHAVIOURAL / IMPLICIT LABELS
  Clicks, dwell time, add-to-cart, purchase, listen-through. Free,
  abundant, reflects real users rather than raters. And badly biased.

POSITION BIAS - the central problem in search ML
  Users click the top result partly because it is on top. So click data
  says "whatever was ranked first is good". Train on it naively and the
  model learns to reproduce the previous ranking. The rich get richer,
  the tail starves, and your offline metric improves the whole time.

  Mitigations, in increasing order of effort:
    - CLICK MODELS that separate examination from relevance: the
      position-based model, the cascade model, dynamic Bayesian network.
      The idea is p(click) = p(examined | position) x p(relevant).
    - INVERSE PROPENSITY SCORING: weight each observed click by
      1/p(examined), so a click at position 10 counts for much more
      than a click at position 1.
    - RANDOMISATION: perturb rankings on a slice of traffic to collect
      genuinely unbiased data. Costs a little relevance, buys the only
      clean labels you will ever have.

  Related biases worth naming: trust bias (users trust top results and
  click them even when poor), presentation bias (bigger images and
  richer snippets get clicked), and selection bias (you only ever learn
  about documents you actually showed).

THE PRACTICAL ANSWER is usually both: human judgements as the stable,
unbiased backbone especially for the tail, behavioural data for scale
and for head queries, and a measured correlation between the two.


#######################################################################
PART 10 - MEASUREMENT
#######################################################################

OFFLINE METRICS

  Recall@k        share of relevant documents that survive retrieval.
                  Measure this on the RETRIEVAL stage separately. It is
                  the ceiling on everything downstream.
  Precision@k     share of the top k that are relevant.
  MRR             1 / rank of the first relevant result, averaged. The
                  right metric for known-item and navigational search,
                  where there is exactly one correct answer.
  NDCG@k          the standard for graded relevance.

  NDCG intuition, which is all you need:
      DCG sums the relevance of each result, DISCOUNTED by how far down
      it sits - position 1 counts fully, position 10 barely counts.
      Then NORMALISE by the best possible ordering for that query, so
      queries with many relevant documents do not dominate the average.
      Result: a 0-to-1 number per query that rewards putting the good
      things high.

          DCG@k = SUM  rel_i / log2(i + 1)
          NDCG@k = DCG@k / IDCG@k

THE WARNING THAT MATTERS: NDCG computed on CLICK labels will cheerfully
greenlight a model that loses money, because it is measuring agreement
with past clicks, not value. Offline metrics are a filter for obviously
bad changes, never a substitute for an online test.

ONLINE METRICS

  Primary should be success-shaped, never engagement-shaped:
    successful sessions, satisfied clicks (a click followed by long
    dwell or a completion), conversion, listen-through, revenue per
    search.

  Diagnostics that separate genuine satisfaction from thrashing:
    pogo-sticking      click then immediately back to results
    reformulation rate user rewrites the query
    clicks per session UP can mean hunting, not engagement
    abandonment rate   searched, clicked nothing
    zero-result rate
    time to first successful click
    depth of clicked rank

  CTR as a primary metric is how you get clickbait. It belongs in the
  diagnostics list, never at the top.

INTERLEAVING - the search-specific experiment technique
  Instead of splitting users between ranker A and ranker B, MIX the two
  rankings into one list (team-draft interleaving) and see which
  ranker's contributed documents get clicked more. Every user sees
  both, so each user is their own control and within-user variance
  disappears. It is typically 10x to 100x more sensitive than a
  standard A/B test, meaning you can read a result in days instead of
  weeks.
  Limit: it compares RANKERS on click-based preference. It cannot
  measure long-term or downstream business effects, so the usual
  pattern is interleaving to select among candidates quickly, then a
  conventional A/B on the winner to measure real impact.


#######################################################################
PART 11 - THE INDEXING PIPELINE
#######################################################################

Half of search engineering, and the half that never gets mentioned by
weak candidates.

    documents -> parse -> enrich -> analyse -> build -> deploy -> serve

  - FULL REBUILD vs INCREMENTAL UPDATE. Rebuilds are simple and slow;
    incremental is fast and accumulates inconsistency.
  - FRESHNESS SLA. How long between a document changing and search
    reflecting it? Minutes for a marketplace, days for a static
    catalog. This is a product decision with a big cost attached.
  - DELETIONS AND TOMBSTONES. An out-of-stock or removed item still
    sitting in the index is one of the most common and most damaging
    search bugs, because it converts at zero.
  - INDEX/SERVING SKEW. The document as indexed versus the document as
    it is now.
  - SHARDING AND REPLICATION. Shard for index size, replicate for QPS
    and availability.
  - CACHING. The head of the query distribution is small and repetitive,
    so a query-result cache buys a lot. The hard part is invalidation
    when the index changes.


#######################################################################
PART 12 - FAILURE MODES TO HAVE READY
#######################################################################

  ZERO RESULTS            no candidates at all. Needs a recall ladder:
                          relax filters, drop terms, spell-correct,
                          fall back to semantic, finally show something.
  NAVIGATIONAL REGRESSION exact match deranked below something more
                          clickable. Catastrophic, very visible, and
                          the signature failure of engagement training.
  VOCABULARY MISMATCH     see Part 4.
  POPULARITY LOOP         behavioural features plus position bias means
                          the already-popular stay popular and the tail
                          is never shown, so never gets data, so never
                          ranks. Self-reinforcing.
  STALE INDEX             results for things that no longer exist or
                          have changed price, stock or title.
  FRESHNESS VS RELEVANCE  a permanent tension with no correct answer,
                          only a chosen trade.
  LATENCY BLOWOUT         usually the re-ranking stage or an ANN
                          parameter turned toward recall.
  MULTILINGUAL / CODE-MIX transliteration, mixed scripts, and a
                          tokeniser that was not built for either.
  LONG-TAIL STARVATION    the aggregate metric looks fine while the
                          tail, which is most of your distinct queries,
                          is quietly broken.


#######################################################################
HOW THIS CONNECTS TO THE REST OF THE REPO
#######################################################################

  - The funnel (cheap over many, expensive over few) is identical in
    recommendation/. Same constraint, different input: a query instead
    of a user.
  - Position bias and the popularity feedback loop are the SAME problem
    as in recommendation/, and the mitigations transfer exactly.
  - "Do not optimise engagement when you are paid in purchases" is the
    Goodhart failure from the RCA repo, arriving here as NDCG-on-clicks.
  - Choosing a relevance threshold, or where to cut the candidate set,
    is the same cost-ratio question as the newsvendor quantile in
    forecasting/: what does a miss cost versus a false positive.
