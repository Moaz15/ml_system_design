INVERTED INDEX - THE CONCEPT
============================

  1. An inverted index is a dictionary: term -> list of documents
     containing it. Nothing more mysterious than that. All the
     engineering is about making that list small and fast to walk.

  2. The posting lists are SORTED BY DOCUMENT ID. That one choice is
     what makes intersection, skipping and compression all possible.

  3. The analyser that builds the index and the analyser that processes
     the query must be the SAME analyser. The classic silent bug.

  4. An index is not updated in place. It is written once as an
     immutable segment, deletes are tombstones, and merges clean up
     later. Freshness is a consequence of that design, not a setting.

  5. The index knows about WORDS, not MEANING. "scary audiobook" will
     never match a show described as "chilling horror story". That gap
     is exactly why vector retrieval exists.


PART 0 - THE RUNNING EXAMPLE
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

A tiny audio catalog of five shows. This is the whole world for the
rest of the file.

  D1  "Mirzapur crime thriller"
  D2  "Thriller night ghost story"
  D3  "Crime Patrol true crime stories"
  D4  "Mirzapur season two thriller"
  D5  "Ghost stories for kids"

Keep these five in mind. Every structure below is built from them, so
you can always check the machine by hand.

PART 1 - THE PROBLEM: SCANNING DOES NOT SCALE
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

The naive way to answer "mirzapur thriller" is to open every document
and look for the words. For five documents that is fine. For ten
million it is not.

  10,000,000 documents  x  ~2KB of text each  =  20 GB to read
  Even at 1 GB/s that is 20 seconds. Per query.

And it gets worse with traffic: every query rereads the same 20 GB.

The fix is the standard computer-science move: PRECOMPUTE. Do the
expensive reading once, at index time, and store the answer in a shape
that makes query time cheap. The shape is the inverted index.

The trade you are making, which is the trade behind every index ever
built:

  index time   slow, offline, once per document, nobody is waiting
  query time   fast, online, millions of times, a user is waiting

Anything you can move from the right column to the left, you move.


#######################################################################
PART 2 - THE IDEA: TURN THE BOOK INSIDE OUT
#######################################################################

A book has a forward map: page -> words on that page. Nobody reads a
book that way to find a topic. They use the index at the back, which
is the reverse map: word -> pages it appears on.

That is the entire idea, and that is why it is called INVERTED - it is
the inversion of the natural document -> terms direction.

  FORWARD INDEX (the natural direction)
    D1 -> {mirzapur, crime, thriller}
    D2 -> {thriller, night, ghost, story}
    ...

  INVERTED INDEX (the useful direction)
    mirzapur  -> [D1, D4]
    thriller  -> [D1, D2, D4]
    crime     -> [D1, D3]
    ghost     -> [D2, D5]
    story     -> [D2, D3, D5]
    ...

Now "mirzapur thriller" is: fetch two short lists, intersect them, get
[D1, D4]. No document was read. Nothing was scanned. The cost is
proportional to how many documents contain the query terms, not to how
many documents exist.

That sentence is the payoff of the whole file. Everything after this is
detail about making those lists small, sorted, skippable and fresh.


#######################################################################
PART 3 - ANALYSIS: FROM TEXT TO TERMS
#######################################################################

Before anything can be indexed you must decide what a "term" is. The
pipeline that turns a string into terms is called ANALYSIS, and it runs
twice: once over every document at index time, once over the query at
query time.

Walk D5 through it: "Ghost stories for kids"

  1. CHARACTER FILTERING   strip HTML, fix encodings, unify unicode
                           (so the two ways to write "e-acute" become
                           one way)

  2. TOKENISATION          "Ghost stories for kids"
                           -> ["Ghost", "stories", "for", "kids"]
                           Splitting on spaces is a convenience of
                           English. Chinese and Japanese have no
                           spaces; Hindi-English code-mixing breaks
                           naive rules; "C++" and "AT&T" die here.

  3. LOWERCASING           -> ["ghost", "stories", "for", "kids"]
                           Cheap and almost always right. Loses the
                           distinction between "Apple" and "apple".

  4. STOP WORDS            drop "for"
                           -> ["ghost", "stories", "kids"]
                           Saves space, but destroys "The Who" and
                           "to be or not to be". Modern systems mostly
                           keep stop words and let scoring handle them,
                           because IDF already makes them nearly
                           worthless. See Part 9.

  5. STEMMING / LEMMA      "stories" -> "story", "kids" -> "kid"
                           -> ["ghost", "story", "kid"]
                           Stemming is crude rule-chopping (fast,
                           sometimes wrong: "university" -> "univers").
                           Lemmatisation uses a dictionary (slower,
                           correct). Both exist to make the query word
                           and the document word collide.

  6. SYNONYMS              optionally "audiobook" -> "audio book"
                           Do this at INDEX time or at QUERY time, not
                           both, or you get a combinatorial mess.

The result for the catalog:

  D1 -> mirzapur, crime, thriller
  D2 -> thriller, night, ghost, story
  D3 -> crime, patrol, true, crime, story
  D4 -> mirzapur, season, two, thriller
  D5 -> ghost, story, kid

THE BUG THAT EVERY SEARCH ENGINEER HAS SHIPPED ONCE
Index the documents with a stemmer, then forget the stemmer on the
query path. The index contains "story". The user types "stories". The
query term is "stories". Nothing matches. No error, no log line, no
crash - just zero results for a chunk of the query distribution, and
nobody notices for a month. Same analyser, both sides, always.


#######################################################################
PART 4 - ANATOMY: DICTIONARY + POSTING LISTS
#######################################################################

The index is two pieces that live in different places.

  THE TERM DICTIONARY (or vocabulary, or lexicon)
    Every distinct term, sorted, each with a pointer to where its list
    lives and a count of how many documents contain it.
    Small enough to keep in memory. Usually a sorted structure that
    supports prefix lookup: a B-tree, a trie, or in Lucene an FST
    (finite state transducer, which is a trie with shared suffixes -
    very compact).

  THE POSTING LISTS
    For each term, the list of documents containing it. Big. Lives on
    disk, read in blocks, memory-mapped and cached by the OS.

For our catalog, with N = 5 documents:

    TERM        df   POSTING LIST (sorted by doc id)
    --------------------------------------------------
    crime        2   [D1, D3]
    ghost        2   [D2, D5]
    kid          1   [D5]
    mirzapur     2   [D1, D4]
    night        1   [D2]
    patrol       1   [D3]
    season       1   [D4]
    story        3   [D2, D3, D5]
    thriller     3   [D1, D2, D4]
    true         1   [D3]
    two          1   [D4]

  df = DOCUMENT FREQUENCY, in how many documents the term appears.
       Stored in the dictionary because scoring needs it constantly
       (Part 9) and it is one number per term, not per posting.

DOC IDs ARE INTERNAL, DENSE INTEGERS
D1..D5 here, but in reality 0,1,2,...,N-1 assigned by the indexer, with
a side table mapping them to real product ids or URLs. Dense small
integers matter: they compress well (Part 10) and they sort cheaply.

WHY SORTED BY DOC ID - the choice everything depends on
  - two sorted lists intersect in one linear pass (Part 6)
  - sorted ascending integers turn into small gaps, which compress
    brutally well (Part 10)
  - you can skip forward without decoding everything (Part 7)
None of that works on an unsorted list. This is not a detail.


#######################################################################
PART 5 - WHAT ELSE LIVES IN A POSTING
#######################################################################

A posting does not have to be just a doc id. Four levels, each costing
more space and buying more capability.

  LEVEL 1  DOC IDS ONLY                  thriller -> [1, 2, 4]
           Answers: does this document contain the term.
           Enough for filtering. Not enough for ranking.

  LEVEL 2  + TERM FREQUENCY (tf)         crime -> [(1,1), (3,2)]
           How often the term occurs in that document. "crime" twice
           in D3, once in D1. Needed by TF-IDF and BM25.

  LEVEL 3  + POSITIONS                   where in the document
           D1: crime at position 2
           D3: crime at positions 1 and 4
           Needed for phrase and proximity queries (Part 8). This is
           usually the single biggest part of the index - positions
           outweigh doc ids and frequencies combined.

  LEVEL 4  + PAYLOADS / OFFSETS          character offsets for
           highlighting, per-term weights, field ids

Our catalog with positions, fully written out. Note the hole at
position 3 in D5, where the stop word "for" was dropped - the position
is burned, not reused, so that the phrase "stories for kids" cannot be
accidentally formed by "stories kids":

    crime     -> (D1, tf 1, [2]) (D3, tf 2, [1,4])
    ghost     -> (D2, tf 1, [3]) (D5, tf 1, [1])
    kid       -> (D5, tf 1, [4])
    mirzapur  -> (D1, tf 1, [1]) (D4, tf 1, [1])
    night     -> (D2, tf 1, [2])
    patrol    -> (D3, tf 1, [2])
    season    -> (D4, tf 1, [2])
    story     -> (D2, tf 1, [4]) (D3, tf 1, [5]) (D5, tf 1, [2])
    thriller  -> (D1, tf 1, [3]) (D2, tf 1, [1]) (D4, tf 1, [4])
    true      -> (D3, tf 1, [3])
    two       -> (D4, tf 1, [3])

The decision of WHICH LEVEL to store is a real design choice. Positions
can double or triple index size. If you never run phrase queries on a
field, do not index positions for it.


#######################################################################
PART 6 - ANSWERING A QUERY: BOOLEAN AND INTERSECTION
#######################################################################

Query: "mirzapur thriller", treated as AND.

  1. Analyse the query with the SAME analyser -> [mirzapur, thriller]
  2. Dictionary lookup -> two posting lists
       mirzapur  [1, 4]      df 2
       thriller  [1, 2, 4]   df 3
  3. Intersect.

THE INTERSECTION, which is the inner loop of all of search:

    i, j at the front of both lists
    while both have elements:
        if list_a[i] == list_b[j]:  emit, advance both
        elif list_a[i] <  list_b[j]: advance i
        else:                        advance j

    mirzapur:  1        4
    thriller:  1   2    4
               ^
    1 == 1 -> emit 1, advance both
    4 vs 2  -> advance thriller
    4 == 4 -> emit 4
    result: [1, 4]  =  D1, D4

Linear in the length of the lists, no document touched. For OR you
merge instead of intersect; for NOT you walk the list and skip matches.

ONE OPTIMISATION WORTH KNOWING, because it is the kind of thing that
separates reading about search from having built it: process the
RAREST TERM FIRST. Intersecting [1,4] with a million-long list costs at
most a few million steps if you drive the loop from the short list and
skip through the long one. Start from the long list and you have
wasted the day. This is why df is kept in the dictionary - it lets the
query planner order the terms before any list is read.


#######################################################################
PART 7 - SKIP POINTERS
#######################################################################

Our lists have three elements. Real ones have ten million. Advancing
one posting at a time through a ten-million-long list to find doc 8,000
is silly, so posting lists carry SKIP POINTERS: every k postings, a
shortcut saying "the next entry here is doc X, and it lives at byte
offset Y".

    thriller: [3, 9, 17, | 41, 55, 60, | 72, 88, 91, | 103, ...]
               skip-> 41       skip-> 72      skip-> 103

Looking for doc 72: instead of stepping 3, 9, 17, 41, 55, 60, 72, you
read the skip entries, see 41 < 72 and 72 <= 72, jump straight to the
third block, decode only it.

  - Typical k is around sqrt(list length), balancing the cost of
    storing skips against the saving from using them.
  - Skips only work because the list is sorted. Again.
  - The operation the whole engine is built around is not "next" but
    ADVANCE-TO(doc_id) - "give me the first posting at or after this
    doc". Everything (intersection, filtering, WAND in Part 11) is
    expressed in terms of it.


#######################################################################
PART 8 - PHRASE AND PROXIMITY QUERIES
#######################################################################

Query: "crime thriller" as an exact phrase.

Doc-id intersection says D1 (D3 has crime but no thriller). But a
phrase needs the words ADJACENT, in order. That is what positions are
for:

    D1:  crime at [2],  thriller at [3]
         3 == 2 + 1  ->  the phrase occurs. MATCH.

Try a phrase that should fail, "thriller night", against D4:

    D4:  thriller at [4],  night - not present. No match.

And against D2:
    D2:  thriller at [1],  night at [2]   ->  2 == 1 + 1. MATCH.

So a phrase query is a two-level intersection: first on doc ids, then
on positions within each surviving document.

PROXIMITY is the same with a looser rule: "crime NEAR/3 story" means
some position of crime and some position of story differ by at most 3.
In D3, crime is at [1,4] and story at [5]: 5 - 4 = 1, so it matches,
and it matches more tightly than a document where the words are 20
words apart. Many rankers feed that distance in as a feature.

SHINGLES (an alternative) index word pairs as single terms - "crime
thriller" becomes one term. Phrase lookup becomes a plain dictionary
hit, very fast, but the index grows a lot and only fixed-length phrases
work. Used for hot phrases, not generally.


#######################################################################
PART 9 - SCORING: WHY THE INDEX STORES tf AND df
#######################################################################

Boolean matching gives you a SET. Users need a RANKED LIST. The index
stores exactly the numbers needed to compute a score cheaply while
walking the postings.

Three intuitions, each one turning into a term in the formula:

  1. A word that occurs often IN THIS DOCUMENT suggests the document
     is about it.                                          -> tf
  2. A word that occurs in NEARLY EVERY DOCUMENT tells you nothing.
     "story" is weak, "mirzapur" is decisive.              -> idf
  3. A long document contains more of every word by accident and must
     not get credit for that.              -> length normalisation

IDF, worked on our catalog (N = 5), using idf = ln(N / df):

    mirzapur   df 2  ->  ln(2.5)  = 0.92     strong signal
    crime      df 2  ->  ln(2.5)  = 0.92
    thriller   df 3  ->  ln(1.67) = 0.51     weaker, it is everywhere
    story      df 3  ->  ln(1.67) = 0.51
    kid        df 1  ->  ln(5)    = 1.61     very rare, very decisive

Notice what this does for free: if "the" appeared in all five
documents, its idf would be ln(1) = 0. Zero. A stop word list is
partly redundant with IDF, which is why modern engines mostly stopped
removing stop words.

BM25 is the same three intuitions with two fixes, and it is the
default lexical scorer everywhere:

    score(q, d) = SUM over terms t in q of

                                     tf x (k1 + 1)
                    idf(t)  x  --------------------------------
                               tf + k1 x (1 - b + b x dl/avgdl)

  FIX ONE - SATURATION (k1, typically 1.2 to 2.0)
    In raw TF-IDF, a document saying "crime" 50 times scores 50x one
    saying it once. That is wrong; the 50th occurrence adds almost
    nothing. BM25 makes tf saturate - the curve flattens.

  FIX TWO - LENGTH (b, typically 0.75)
    dl/avgdl is this document's length over the average. b = 1 fully
    penalises long documents, b = 0 ignores length entirely, 0.75 is
    the usual compromise.

Our document lengths, in indexed terms:
    D1 = 3, D2 = 4, D3 = 5, D4 = 4, D5 = 3    avgdl = 3.8

So the index stores tf per posting, df per term, and dl per document
(in the forward index, Part 13) - because those are exactly the three
inputs BM25 needs, and they can all be read while walking the list.
The structure is shaped by the formula.

BM25F is the field-aware version: a term matching in the TITLE should
count more than one matching in the DESCRIPTION. It combines the
per-field frequencies with per-field weights BEFORE saturating, which
is subtly but importantly different from scoring the fields separately
and adding up.


#######################################################################
PART 10 - COMPRESSION: WHY THE LISTS ARE SMALL
#######################################################################

Posting lists are the bulk of the index and they are read from disk on
every query, so every byte saved is latency saved. Three tricks, in
order of how much they buy.

  GAP (DELTA) ENCODING - the big one
    A sorted list of doc ids becomes a list of differences.

      thriller: [3, 9, 17, 41, 55, 60, 72]
      becomes:  [3, 6,  8, 24, 14,  5, 12]

    Large numbers become small numbers. And a common term has a DENSE
    list - it appears in many documents, so consecutive doc ids are
    close together, so the gaps are tiny. The longer the list, the
    better it compresses. That is a very friendly property.

  VARIABLE-BYTE / VARINT ENCODING
    Do not spend 4 bytes on the number 6. Use one byte, with the top
    bit saying "there is more". Small gaps become one byte each.
    Fancier schemes (PForDelta, Simple-9, Roaring bitmaps, FOR with
    SIMD) pack blocks of 128 gaps together and decode them with vector
    instructions. Same idea, more engineering.

  FRONT CODING in the dictionary
    Sorted terms share prefixes: "thrill", "thriller", "thrillers".
    Store the shared prefix once and only the suffixes. An FST does
    this and shares suffixes too.

Rough result: a well-built inverted index with positions is on the
order of 20-40% the size of the original text, and doc-ids-only can be
under 10%. That is why the whole thing fits in memory often enough to
make search feel instant.


#######################################################################
PART 11 - TOP-K WITHOUT SCORING EVERYTHING
#######################################################################

Query "thriller" against ten million documents. Two million of them
contain it. The user wants 10 results. Scoring two million documents to
return ten is almost all wasted work.

  MAX-SCORE / WAND (Weak AND) - the standard answer
    For each term you can precompute an UPPER BOUND on what it could
    ever contribute to any document's score (its idf times the best
    possible tf term). While walking, you keep a heap of the best k so
    far; the kth score is your THRESHOLD. If the sum of the upper
    bounds of the terms a candidate could match is below the
    threshold, that candidate cannot make the top k, so you do not
    score it - you ADVANCE-TO past it. Whole regions of the posting
    lists are skipped without being decoded.

  BLOCK-MAX WAND
    The same, but bounds are stored per block of postings rather than
    per whole list, so the bounds are much tighter and much more gets
    skipped. This is what a modern Lucene actually runs.

  IMPACT-ORDERED / TIERED POSTINGS
    Sort a list by score contribution instead of by doc id, read the
    front of it, stop early. You get speed and lose the clean sorted
    order that intersection needs, so it is used selectively.

  STATIC TIERING
    Keep a small index of high-quality documents (popular, recent,
    good). Query it first; if it yields enough good results, never
    touch the big index. A large share of head traffic never leaves
    tier one.

The theme: these are all the same move as the funnel in
search/concept.txt - do the cheap thing over many, the expensive thing
over few - applied one level deeper, inside retrieval itself.


#######################################################################
PART 12 - UPDATES, DELETES, SEGMENTS AND MERGES
#######################################################################

Here is the part that surprises people. You cannot efficiently update
an inverted index in place. Adding one document to the catalog would
mean inserting into the middle of dozens of compressed, sorted,
skip-pointered lists on disk. Every one of them would have to be
rewritten.

So nobody does that. Instead:

  SEGMENTS
    A segment is a small, complete, IMMUTABLE inverted index. New
    documents accumulate in memory and are periodically flushed as a
    new segment. An index is therefore a collection of segments, and a
    query runs against ALL of them and merges the results.

  DELETES ARE TOMBSTONES
    You cannot remove a posting from an immutable segment. You set a
    bit in a deleted-documents bitmap, and results are filtered against
    it at query time. The data is still there, taking space, until:

  MERGES
    In the background, small segments are merged into bigger ones.
    During the merge, deleted documents are genuinely dropped and the
    lists are rewritten compactly. Merging is expensive IO and is a
    common cause of latency spikes and disk-usage sawtooth.

  AN UPDATE IS A DELETE PLUS AN INSERT
    There is no edit. Changing a title tombstones the old document and
    indexes a new one, with a new internal doc id.

  REFRESH VS COMMIT (near-real-time search)
    REFRESH makes recent documents searchable by opening a new
    in-memory segment - typically every second or so. COMMIT makes
    them durable on disk - slower, rarer. "Why is my new show not
    showing up" is almost always the refresh interval, and the knob
    trades freshness against indexing throughput.

This is the same log-structured design as an LSM-tree database, for the
same reason: sequential writes are cheap, random in-place updates to
compressed sorted structures are not.

CONSEQUENCE WORTH SAYING OUT LOUD
Because df and N change as segments change, scores change slightly as
merges happen, and two shards with different document mixes compute
slightly different idf for the same term. Usually ignorable, sometimes
the explanation for "the same query gives different orders on different
replicas".


#######################################################################
PART 13 - THE FORWARD INDEX, THE OTHER HALF
#######################################################################

The inverted index answers "which documents contain this term". It is
hopeless at the opposite question - "for this document, what is its
price / rating / length / category" - which you need for sorting,
filtering, faceting and feature extraction.

So every real engine keeps BOTH directions:

  INVERTED INDEX   term -> documents            matching
  DOC VALUES       document -> field value      sorting, faceting,
                   (column-oriented, per field) scoring features
  STORED FIELDS    document -> original text    display on the results
                                                page, highlighting

Doc values are stored column-wise because the access pattern is "read
this one field for a thousand documents", not "read everything about
one document". Document length (dl) for BM25 lives here too.

If a question asks how you would rank by price, or facet by category,
or feed a feature into a learned ranker, the answer involves doc values,
not posting lists.


#######################################################################
PART 14 - FIELDS, FILTERS AND FACETS
#######################################################################

Documents are not flat text. A show has a title, a description, a
language, a category, a rating.

  FIELDED TERMS
    The term is really (field, text). "title:mirzapur" and
    "description:mirzapur" are different dictionary entries with
    different posting lists. That is what lets the title match count
    more (BM25F, Part 9).

  FILTERS ARE JUST POSTING LISTS
    "language = hindi" is a term with a posting list. Filtering is
    intersecting with it. For very common filters the list is better
    stored as a BITMAP (one bit per document, or a Roaring bitmap),
    because AND-ing two bitmaps is a few machine instructions per 64
    documents.

  FILTERS ARE CACHEABLE, QUERIES ARE NOT
    "language = hindi" is the same bitmap for every user, so it is
    cached and reused. The scored part of the query is not. Separating
    the two - a filter clause that does not affect score and a query
    clause that does - is a real performance lever.

  FACET COUNTS ("Thriller (412)") come from counting over the matching
    doc set using doc values, not from the inverted index.


#######################################################################
PART 15 - PREFIX, WILDCARD AND FUZZY
#######################################################################

The dictionary is sorted, which gives you more than exact lookup.

  PREFIX / AUTOCOMPLETE     "thri*" is a range scan over the sorted
                            dictionary: every term between "thri" and
                            "thrj". Cheap. An FST does it in memory.

  LEADING WILDCARD          "*hriller" cannot use the sort order at
                            all. The fix is a second index over
                            REVERSED terms, so a leading wildcard
                            becomes a trailing one.

  GENERAL WILDCARD / FUZZY  a K-GRAM INDEX: index 3-letter pieces of
                            each term ("thr", "hri", "ril", ...) and
                            look up terms sharing enough pieces.
                            Lucene instead walks the FST against a
                            Levenshtein automaton, which finds all
                            terms within edit distance 1 or 2 without
                            scanning the dictionary.

  SPELL CORRECTION          same machinery: candidate terms within a
                            small edit distance, ranked by df and by
                            what users actually clicked afterwards.

All of these expand ONE query term into MANY index terms, then OR the
posting lists. That is why a sloppy wildcard query can be thousands of
times more expensive than it looks, and why engines cap the expansion.


#######################################################################
PART 16 - SCALE: SHARDING, REPLICATION, CACHING
#######################################################################

  DOCUMENT PARTITIONING (what everyone does)
    Split the documents across shards; each shard holds a complete
    inverted index over its own slice. A query goes to ALL shards,
    each returns its local top k, and a coordinator merges.
    + simple, balanced, easy to add documents
    - every query touches every shard, so tail latency is the SLOWEST
      shard, not the average one

  TERM PARTITIONING (rare)
    Split by term: this node owns "a-f". A query touches only the
    nodes holding its terms.
    + less fan-out per query
    - brutal load imbalance (one hot term = one hot node), and
      multi-term queries must ship long posting lists across the
      network. Almost nobody ships this.

  REPLICATION
    Copies of each shard for QPS and availability. Shard for index
    size, replicate for traffic.

  CACHING, at three levels
    - query result cache: the head of the query distribution is small
      and repetitive, so this buys a lot. The hard part is
      invalidation when the index changes.
    - filter/bitmap cache: shared across users, very high hit rate.
    - OS page cache over the posting files: the quiet one that does
      most of the work, and the reason a cold node is slow for
      minutes after a restart.


#######################################################################
PART 17 - SIZING INTUITION
#######################################################################

Worth being able to do on a whiteboard.

  10,000,000 documents, ~200 terms each after analysis
  = 2,000,000,000 postings

  doc id + tf, gap-encoded and varint'd:  ~1-2 bytes per posting
      -> roughly 2-4 GB
  with positions:                         ~2-3x that
      -> roughly 6-12 GB

  Distinct terms: a few million (Heaps' law - vocabulary grows roughly
  as the square root of collection size, and never stops growing,
  because names, typos and product codes are infinite).
  The dictionary is therefore small - hundreds of MB at most, and it
  stays in memory.

Two laws worth naming:
  ZIPF   term frequencies are brutally skewed. The few most common
         terms cover a huge share of all occurrences, and their
         posting lists are gigantic - which is exactly why WAND and
         block-max matter.
  HEAPS  vocabulary keeps growing with collection size.


#######################################################################
PART 18 - WHAT AN INVERTED INDEX CANNOT DO
#######################################################################

It matches STRINGS. It has no idea what they mean.

  query: "scary audiobook for a late night"
  doc:   "a chilling horror story to listen to after dark"

  Shared terms after analysis: essentially none. BM25 score: ~0.
  A human would call this a perfect match.

This is VOCABULARY MISMATCH, and no amount of stemming, synonyms or
tuning fixes it in general - you cannot enumerate every way people
phrase things. It is the exact gap that dense/vector retrieval fills:
embed query and document into the same space and retrieve by
similarity, so meaning matches even when words do not.

And the mirror image is just as real:

  query: "B07XJ8C8F5"  (an exact product code)
  A vector index will return things that are vaguely similar. The
  inverted index returns the one right answer, instantly.

Lexical is precise and literal. Vector is fuzzy and semantic. They
fail in OPPOSITE directions, which is why production systems run both
and fuse the results (hybrid retrieval, usually with reciprocal rank
fusion). The inverted index does not become obsolete; it becomes one
of two legs.


#######################################################################
PART 19 - FAILURE MODES TO HAVE READY
#######################################################################

  ANALYSER SKEW        index-time and query-time analysers differ.
                       Silent zero-result class. The classic.
  STALE / LAGGING INDEX documents searchable seconds or hours after
                       they change. Refresh interval, merge pressure,
                       or a stuck indexing pipeline.
  TOMBSTONE BLOAT      heavy deletes without merges: index size and
                       query cost grow while the document count does
                       not.
  HOT TERM             a query term with a 50-million-long posting
                       list. Mitigated by WAND, tiering, or just
                       refusing to score it alone.
  EXPANSION EXPLOSION  a wildcard or fuzzy query expanding to 100,000
                       terms and OR-ing 100,000 posting lists.
  DEEP PAGINATION      "page 500" means every shard must produce and
                       merge 5,000 results. Cost grows with offset.
                       Use search-after/cursors, not offsets.
  SHARD TAIL LATENCY   p99 of the query is p99 of the slowest of N
                       shards, which is much worse than p99 of one.
  IDF SKEW ACROSS SHARDS same query, different order on different
                       replicas, because df is local.
  MISSING FROM INDEX   the item exists in the catalog but was never
                       indexed. Converts at zero and is invisible in
                       every ranking metric, because the ranker never
                       sees it.
  COLD CACHE           a restarted node serving from disk until the
                       page cache refills.


#######################################################################
THE WHOLE STORY IN ONE PAGE
#######################################################################

  1,2   Scanning documents does not scale, so precompute the
        reverse map: term -> documents.
  3     Decide what a term is, and decide it identically on both
        the index side and the query side.
  4     Store a sorted dictionary of terms, each pointing at a
        posting list sorted by doc id.
  5     Put tf and positions in the postings if ranking and phrase
        queries need them.
  6     Answer a query by intersecting sorted lists, rarest first.
  7     Skip through long lists instead of walking them.
  8     Use positions for phrases and proximity.
  9     Score with BM25, which is exactly tf, df and length.
  10    Compress the gaps, because sorted ids make small numbers.
  11    Do not score everything - bound, threshold and skip.
  12    Never update in place: immutable segments, tombstones,
        background merges.
  13    Keep the forward direction too, for sorting and features.
  14    Fields make filters, and filters are cacheable bitmaps.
  15    The sorted dictionary also gives prefix, wildcard and fuzzy.
  16    Shard by document, replicate for traffic, cache at three
        levels.
  18    It still only matches words, so pair it with vectors.
