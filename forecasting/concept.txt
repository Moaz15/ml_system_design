FORECASTING - THE CONCEPTS
==========================
Read this before any case in forecasting/. It is the mental model and
the vocabulary, not a case and not an answer.

Everything below hangs off TWO facts. If you remember nothing else,
remember those two and re-derive the rest.


#######################################################################
IF YOU REMEMBER ONLY FIVE THINGS
#######################################################################

  1. Time is ordered. The future is not exchangeable with the past, so
     every technique that assumes rows are interchangeable - random
     splits, shuffled CV, naive feature engineering - is wrong here.

  2. A forecast is not the product. The DECISION is the product. Find
     the decision, its lead time, and what each direction of error
     costs, before you touch a model.

  3. If the two directions of error cost differently, the answer is a
     QUANTILE, not a mean. Almost every real decision is asymmetric.

  4. Beat seasonal naive or do not ship. It is free, it cannot break,
     and it is far better than people expect.

  5. Every feature must be knowable at forecast time, for the entire
     horizon. The horizon decides which features you are even allowed.


#######################################################################
PART 1 - THE TWO FACTS THAT GENERATE EVERYTHING
#######################################################################

FACT ONE: TIME IS ORDERED.

  Consequences, all of them forced:
    - no random train/test splits, ever - they train on the future
    - backtesting must roll forward through time, imitating production
    - features split into "knowable in advance" and "only observable
      after the fact", and only the first kind survives a long horizon
    - errors compound when you feed predictions back as inputs
    - the data generating process can change (structural breaks), and
      unlike in cross-sectional ML you cannot resample your way out
    - the most recent data is the most relevant AND the least settled

FACT TWO: A FORECAST IS AN INPUT TO A DECISION.

  Consequences:
    - the horizon comes from the decision's lead time, not from taste
    - the grain comes from what the decision is made over
    - the loss function comes from what the two errors cost in money
    - asymmetric cost means you report a quantile, not a mean
    - the headline metric is decision cost, not accuracy
    - if no action changes, do not build it - a dashboard is not a
      decision


#######################################################################
PART 2 - VOCABULARY
#######################################################################

  TARGET          the quantity being predicted. Prefer the quantity the
                  decision is denominated in (bytes if the invoice is in
                  bytes, units if you order units).
  GRAIN           the time step: hourly, daily, weekly, monthly. Also
                  the entity grain: total, per SKU, per city.
  FREQUENCY (m)   observations per seasonal cycle. Daily data with a
                  weekly cycle has m = 7; monthly with yearly, m = 12.
  ORIGIN          the moment you stand at to make the forecast. Also
                  called the forecast origin or cutoff.
  HORIZON (h)     how far ahead. h=1 and h=90 are different problems
                  with different feasible feature sets.
  LEAD TIME       how long before the decision takes effect. This SETS
                  the horizon.
  VINTAGE         which origin a given forecast was made from. Store it.
  IN-SAMPLE       data the model was fit on. OUT-OF-SAMPLE, data after
                  the origin. Only the second means anything.
  POINT FORECAST  a single number. PROBABILISTIC / DISTRIBUTIONAL
                  forecast, a full distribution or a set of quantiles.
  COVARIATE       an input other than the series' own past. Splits into
                  KNOWN-FUTURE (calendar, planned promo, published
                  schedule) and PAST-ONLY (weather actuals, competitor
                  price).
  LOCAL MODEL     one model fitted per series.
  GLOBAL MODEL    one model fitted across many series, sharing
                  parameters. How deep learning gets enough data, and
                  how new series get a forecast at all.
  BACKTEST        evaluating on history by rolling an origin forward.


#######################################################################
PART 3 - WHAT A SERIES IS MADE OF
#######################################################################

The classical decomposition, and the reason it is still useful is that
each component has a different driver and a different failure mode:

    y_t  =  TREND  +  SEASONALITY  +  REMAINDER      (additive)
    y_t  =  TREND  x  SEASONALITY  x  REMAINDER      (multiplicative)

  TREND        slow direction of travel. May be linear, damped, or
               piecewise with changepoints.
  SEASONALITY  a pattern that repeats on a FIXED, known period - day of
               week, month of year. Multiple seasonalities coexist:
               daily data can carry weekly and yearly cycles at once.
  CYCLE        a repeating pattern of NON-fixed period, like a business
               cycle. Different from seasonality; harder; usually
               ignored in practice.
  REMAINDER    what is left. Should look like noise. If it does not,
               there is structure you have not modelled.

ADDITIVE OR MULTIPLICATIVE
  Additive: the seasonal swing is the same SIZE regardless of level
  (+200 units every Saturday). Multiplicative: the swing is the same
  PROPORTION (+15% every Saturday). Growing businesses are almost
  always multiplicative.
  The trick: take logs, and multiplicative becomes additive. Most
  practical work on a growing series happens in log space - but
  remember that the mean of the log is not the log of the mean, so
  back-transforming a forecast needs care.

STL DECOMPOSITION
  Seasonal-Trend decomposition using Loess. Robust, handles changing
  seasonality, gives you the three components to inspect. Worth running
  on any new series before modelling, purely to see what you have.

FOURIER TERMS
  Represent seasonality as sin/cos pairs at the seasonal frequency and
  its harmonics. Elegant when the period is long (365) or when several
  seasonalities overlap, where dummy variables would explode. K pairs
  gives you 2K features and controls how wiggly the seasonal shape is.


#######################################################################
PART 4 - STATIONARITY AND AUTOCORRELATION
#######################################################################

You need this to read classical methods and to talk to people who grew
up on them, even if your production model ends up being a GBDT.

STATIONARITY
  A series is stationary if its statistical properties do not depend on
  when you look - constant mean, constant variance, autocovariance
  depending only on the lag. Trends and seasonality both break it.
  Classical models (ARMA) require it; you get there by DIFFERENCING:

    first difference       y_t - y_{t-1}          removes a linear trend
    seasonal difference    y_t - y_{t-m}          removes seasonality
    log then difference    stabilises variance too

  A RANDOM WALK is the canonical non-stationary series - its best
  forecast is its last value, which is exactly the naive method. Many
  financial series are close to this, which is why beating naive there
  is so hard.

  Tests: ADF (null = non-stationary), KPSS (null = stationary). Run
  both; they disagree in informative ways.

AUTOCORRELATION
  ACF   correlation of the series with itself at lag k. Peaks at
        multiples of the seasonal period reveal seasonality.
  PACF  the same, with the effect of shorter lags removed.

  Reading them for model order:
      AR(p):  PACF cuts off sharply after lag p, ACF decays
      MA(q):  ACF  cuts off sharply after lag q, PACF decays

  After fitting anything, plot the ACF of the RESIDUALS. If structure
  remains, the model has not extracted everything. The Ljung-Box test
  formalises this.


#######################################################################
PART 5 - THE MODEL LADDER
#######################################################################

Climb it. Stop as soon as the decision-cost metric stops improving by
more than the maintenance cost.

  LEVEL 0 - BENCHMARKS (never skip)
    naive            y_hat = y_{t-1}
    seasonal naive   y_hat = y_{t-m}        <- THE baseline
    drift            naive plus the average historical slope
    mean             the historical average
  These take one line each and must appear in every results table.

  LEVEL 1 - EXPONENTIAL SMOOTHING
    SES              weighted average, weights decaying into the past.
                     Level only.
    Holt             adds a trend. Damped Holt adds a damping parameter
                     so the trend flattens out instead of exploding -
                     damped trend is a very strong default.
    Holt-Winters     adds seasonality, additive or multiplicative.
    ETS              the general framework: Error x Trend x Seasonal,
                     each of which can be None / Additive /
                     Multiplicative (plus damped). Fitting picks the
                     combination automatically. Cheap and excellent.

  LEVEL 2 - THETA
    Simple, won the M3 competition, still competitive. Essentially SES
    with drift on a decomposed series. Include it as a benchmark.

  LEVEL 3 - ARIMA FAMILY
    ARIMA(p,d,q)              AR order, differencing, MA order
    SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)_m    the same with seasonal terms
    SARIMAX                   plus exogenous regressors
    Strong on short, clean, single-seasonality series. Weak with
    multiple seasonalities, awkward with many covariates, slow to fit
    at scale. auto_arima will pick orders for you.

  LEVEL 4 - PROPHET
    Trend with automatic changepoints, Fourier seasonality, holiday
    effects, all fitted in a Bayesian framework. Very fast to get
    something reasonable; handles missing data and holidays gracefully.
    Honest caveats: it is a curve-fitter, not a time series model in
    the ARIMA sense; it often loses to a tuned GBDT or even to ETS; its
    intervals are frequently badly calibrated. Good first cut, rarely
    the answer.

  LEVEL 5 - REGRESSION / GBDT ON TABULAR FEATURES
    Reframe as supervised learning (Part 6), then use LightGBM,
    XGBoost or CatBoost with lag and calendar features. Quantiles come
    free via pinball loss. Handles many covariates, many series and
    non-linear interactions. THIS IS THE PRACTICAL WORKHORSE for
    business data, and it won M5 decisively.

  LEVEL 6 - GLOBAL AND DEEP MODELS
    DeepAR           probabilistic RNN, global across series
    N-BEATS/N-HiTS   deep residual stacks, strong on pure series
    TFT              Temporal Fusion Transformer, handles static and
                     time-varying covariates, gives attention-based
                     interpretability
    PatchTST etc.    transformer variants
    These need MANY related series or very long histories. One series
    with a thousand daily points cannot justify them, and saying that
    plainly is a strength.

  LEVEL 7 - FOUNDATION MODELS
    Chronos, TimesFM, Moirai, Lag-Llama, TimeGPT: pretrained on large
    time series corpora, usable zero-shot. Genuinely useful for cold
    start and for a fast baseline across many series. As of now they do
    not reliably beat a well-tuned GBDT on business data with strong
    covariates, so treat them as a strong prior, not a conclusion.

  SPECIAL - INTERMITTENT DEMAND
    Croston's method, SBA, TSB. See Part 13.

WHAT THE M COMPETITIONS ACTUALLY TAUGHT
  M3 (2000)  simple methods and combinations beat complex ones
  M4 (2018)  a hybrid of exponential smoothing and an RNN won;
             combinations dominated the top of the table
  M5 (2020)  Walmart retail data - gradient boosted trees dominated,
             and probabilistic accuracy was scored separately
  The durable lesson: benchmark honestly, combine models, and do not
  assume complexity wins.

ENSEMBLING
  Averaging several reasonable forecasts is one of the most reliable
  free wins in the field. A simple mean of ETS, ARIMA and GBDT often
  beats any of them. Do this before reaching for a bigger model.


#######################################################################
PART 6 - REFRAMING AS A SUPERVISED LEARNING PROBLEM
#######################################################################

The move that lets you use ordinary ML on a time series. Turn the
series into rows:

    target       y at time t+h
    features     lags of y, rolling statistics of y, calendar
                 attributes of t+h, covariates known at origin t

    t      y    lag_7  lag_14  roll_mean_28  dow  is_holiday   y_{t+7}
    ...

Now it is a regression table and LightGBM will take it.

THE RULES THAT KEEP IT HONEST
  - Every feature row must be computable using ONLY information
    available at the origin. Compute rolling statistics with a shift,
    and never over the full series before splitting.
  - The lag must be at least h. For a 7-day-ahead forecast, lag_1 does
    not exist at prediction time. This is the single most common bug in
    tabular forecasting, and it produces a beautiful backtest and a
    useless production model.
  - Keep the row's timestamp so the backtest can split on it.

WHEN THE TABULAR FRAME IS WRONG
  If there is no history for the entity you are predicting - a brand
  new product, a show not yet made - there are no lags to build, and
  the problem is not a time series problem at all. It is cross-sectional
  regression on metadata and analogues, pooled across entities.
  Recognising this is worth more than any model choice.


#######################################################################
PART 7 - FEATURES AND THE FORECAST-TIME RULE
#######################################################################

THE RULE: will I have this value, for every date in the horizon, at the
moment I produce the forecast? If not, it is leakage.

  KNOWN-FUTURE - safe at any horizon
    day of week, day of month, week of year, month, quarter
    holiday and festival flags, WITH LEAD AND LAG WINDOWS - behaviour
      ramps before a festival and decays after, so a single-day flag
      captures almost none of the effect
    moving holidays that shift each year (Diwali, Eid, Easter, Chinese
      New Year) - these need a maintained calendar table or the model
      silently breaks the same month every year
    long weekends and bridge days, which are not the same as holidays
    school and exam calendars, sports fixtures, published schedules
    planned promotions, planned price changes, planned marketing
    payday effects, month-end and quarter-end
    time index / trend term
    cyclical encodings: sin and cos of the position in the cycle, so
      that December is adjacent to January

  PAST-ONLY - safe only at short horizons
    lags and rolling means, standard deviations, min and max
    expanding statistics
    recent level, recent volatility
    exogenous actuals: weather observed, competitor price observed

  PLANNED BUT UNCERTAIN
    budgets and launch plans. Usable, but first regress actual on
    planned historically and look at the spread. A noisy regressor with
    a confident coefficient is worse than no regressor.

THE HORIZON DETERMINES THE FEATURE SET
  At h = 1 to 14, lags carry most of the signal.
  At h = 90, there is no recent observation to lag from, and the model
  is essentially a calendar-and-covariates regression with a trend.
  Two horizon regimes usually means two configurations of one pipeline,
  not one model pretending to serve both.


#######################################################################
PART 8 - MULTI-STEP: DIRECT VERSUS RECURSIVE
#######################################################################

  RECURSIVE (iterative)
    Train one model for h=1. Predict, feed the prediction back in as a
    lag, predict again. Simple, one model. Errors COMPOUND, and the
    model is being fed its own guesses as if they were observations.
    Fine for short horizons, bad past a couple of seasonal cycles.

  DIRECT
    Train a separate model per horizon, or one model with the horizon
    as a feature, each mapping from information-at-origin to y_{t+h}.
    No compounding. More models to maintain, and it cannot exploit the
    sequential structure between steps.

  MULTI-OUTPUT
    One model emitting the whole horizon vector at once. Native to the
    deep models.

  Default: recursive for short, direct for long. Say which you chose
  and why; it is a real design decision, not a detail.


#######################################################################
PART 9 - BACKTESTING
#######################################################################

The one rule: BACKTEST THE WAY YOU WILL FORECAST. Same origin spacing,
same horizon, same information cutoff as production. Anything else
measures a system you are not going to run.

ROLLING-ORIGIN EVALUATION (time series cross-validation, walk-forward)

    train ---------|  test h
    train -------------|  test h
    train -----------------|  test h
                    origins advance

  EXPANDING WINDOW (anchored)   training set grows. Use when the whole
                                history is relevant.
  SLIDING WINDOW (unanchored)   fixed-length training window that moves.
                                Use when the regime changes and old data
                                misleads.

  ORIGIN SPACING must match the decision cadence. If you declare a
  number on the 1st of each month, your origins are the 1st of each
  month - which over three years gives you about 36 evaluation points.
  Knowing how few origins you have is itself a finding: it limits how
  confidently you can pick between models.

  GAP / EMBARGO. If a feature uses a window that straddles the split,
  or if the label needs time to settle, insert a gap between train and
  test so nothing leaks across.

  DATA SETTLEMENT. If recent partitions are still being revised, the
  backtest must use the data AS IT LOOKED at the origin, not as it
  looks now. Otherwise production sees worse inputs than the backtest
  did and underperforms for reasons nobody can find.

COMPARING TWO MODELS
  With 30-odd origins you are working with a small sample. Use the
  Diebold-Mariano test, or a block bootstrap over origins, before
  declaring a winner. A raw mean difference over 30 points will pick
  noise and you will defend it for a year.


#######################################################################
PART 10 - METRICS
#######################################################################

SCALE-DEPENDENT (comparable only within one series)
  MAE    mean absolute error. Optimised by the MEDIAN.
  RMSE   root mean squared error. Optimised by the MEAN. Punishes large
         errors harder; sensitive to outliers.
  Choosing between them is already a statement about what you want.

PERCENTAGE
  MAPE   mean absolute percentage error. Popular and flawed:
           - undefined and explosive as the actual approaches zero
           - an under-forecast's error is capped at 100% while an
             over-forecast is unbounded, so minimising MAPE
             systematically biases the forecast LOW
           - meaningless on series that cross zero
  sMAPE  the "symmetric" fix, which is not actually symmetric and is
         unstable near zero. Used in competitions; avoid in production.

SCALED - the good ones
  MASE   mean absolute error divided by the in-sample MAE of the
         seasonal naive forecast.
           MASE < 1  means better than seasonal naive
           MASE = 1  means you built seasonal naive the hard way
         Scale free, comparable across series, undefined only for a
         flat series, and it answers the question you actually care
         about. Prefer it as the point-accuracy metric.
  RMSSE  the squared analogue, used in M5.

PROBABILISTIC
  PINBALL (quantile) LOSS  for a target quantile tau:
           (y - yhat) * tau        if y >= yhat   (under-forecast)
           (yhat - y) * (1 - tau)  if y <  yhat   (over-forecast)
         Minimised exactly at the tau-quantile. This is both the metric
         AND the training loss for quantile models.
  CRPS   continuous ranked probability score - the integral of pinball
         loss across all quantiles. One number for a whole predictive
         distribution.
  COVERAGE / CALIBRATION  over the backtest, is the 90% interval
         actually containing the truth about 90% of the time? An
         uncalibrated interval is worse than none, because it gets
         acted on.

THE METRIC THAT OUTRANKS ALL OF THEM
  DECISION COST. Replay the real decision over the backtest and price
  it in money, under each candidate model AND under the incumbent -
  including the incumbent human with a spreadsheet, which is a model
  and deserves a row in the table. "This saves Rs X a year" is the only
  metric a business can argue with, and the only one that can tell you
  a more accurate model is not worth shipping.


#######################################################################
PART 11 - PROBABILISTIC FORECASTING AND PICKING THE QUANTILE
#######################################################################

WHY: the decision is asymmetric. Running out costs something different
from having too much. The mean is optimal only when the two costs are
equal, which is almost never.

HOW TO GET A DISTRIBUTION
  - empirical residual quantiles from the backtest, per horizon
    (errors grow with h, so the interval must widen with h)
  - quantile regression / GBDT trained with pinball loss at each
    quantile you need
  - parametric intervals from a statistical model (ETS, ARIMA) - easy,
    but they assume a distribution and are usually too narrow
  - simulation: sample paths forward from a generative model
  - bootstrapping residuals
  - conformal prediction, adapted for the non-exchangeable case
    (EnbPI, adaptive conformal inference) - gives coverage guarantees
    with weak assumptions

  Watch for quantile crossing when you fit quantiles independently -
  the P90 can come out below the P80. Sort, or fit jointly.

PICKING THE QUANTILE - THE NEWSVENDOR / CRITICAL FRACTILE

  Let Cu be the cost of being ONE UNIT SHORT and Co the cost of being
  ONE UNIT LONG. The optimal service level is

                        Cu
        tau*  =  ---------------
                    Cu  +  Co

  and you forecast the tau* quantile.

  Worked, from case 01: commit capacity at Rs 1.60/GB, overage billed
  at Rs 3.52/GB, unused commitment not refunded.
      being short one GB costs the premium   3.52 - 1.60 = 1.92
      being long one GB costs the waste                  = 1.60
      tau* = 1.92 / (1.92 + 1.60) = 0.545   ->  commit the P55

  The counterintuitive part, and the reason this is worth memorising:
  a penalty multiplier of 2.2x lands you barely above the median, not
  far out in the tail. Meanwhile a decision where running short costs
  retention or safety can easily give Cu/Co of 50:1, which is P98.
  SAME SERIES, OPPOSITE POSTURES. If one number is serving two such
  decisions, it is failing at both.

  Caveats: the formula assumes linear costs on both sides and no
  salvage value. Contracts with minimum commitments, tiered rates or
  true-ups need the expected-cost curve computed directly.

THIS GENERALISES FAR BEYOND FORECASTING
  Any threshold choice is this calculation: fraud block cutoffs,
  moderation auto-remove thresholds, inventory levels, staffing,
  alerting. Whenever you are about to pick a threshold by eye or report
  a mean, ask what the two errors cost.


#######################################################################
PART 12 - HIERARCHIES AND RECONCILIATION
#######################################################################

Forecasts are usually consumed at several levels at once - SKU,
category, region, national - and the levels must ADD UP. Independent
forecasts will not.

  BOTTOM-UP     forecast the leaves, sum upward. Coherent by
                construction. Suffers when the leaves are noisy or
                intermittent.
  TOP-DOWN      forecast the total, split by historical proportions.
                Stable at the top, loses leaf-level signal, and the
                proportions themselves drift.
  MIDDLE-OUT    forecast a middle level, aggregate up and split down.
  OPTIMAL RECONCILIATION (MinT and relatives)
                forecast every level independently, then project the
                whole vector onto the coherent subspace, weighting by
                the forecast error covariance. Usually the best
                accuracy, and it lets each level use whatever model
                suits it.

  NON-NESTED HIERARCHIES. Real hierarchies often overlap rather than
  nest - age x city x genre, where a row belongs to several groupings
  at once. These are "grouped" rather than strictly hierarchical
  structures and reconciliation still applies, but the constraint
  matrix is bigger and top-down is not even definable.

  AGGREGATION HELPS ACCURACY. A total is nearly always easier to
  forecast than its parts, because idiosyncratic noise cancels. If the
  decision only needs the total, do not forecast the parts.


#######################################################################
PART 13 - THE HARD CASES
#######################################################################

INTERMITTENT AND LUMPY DEMAND
  Series that are mostly zeros with occasional spikes - spare parts,
  long-tail SKUs, small sellers. The mean is meaningless: a forecast of
  0.3 units is never a possible outcome.
    Croston's method  decompose into demand SIZE and the INTERVAL
                      between demands, smooth each separately
    SBA               corrects Croston's known upward bias
    TSB               updates the PROBABILITY of demand rather than the
                      interval, so it handles items going obsolete
    Better still      forecast the full distribution, or model
                      p(demand occurs) x size | occurs, and let the
                      decision read the quantile it needs

COLD START - NO HISTORY
  A new product, a new store, a show not yet made. Options, in order:
    - a global model pooled across similar series, so the new one
      inherits the shared shape
    - cross-sectional regression on metadata and analogues (the series
      framing is simply wrong here)
    - a matched-analogue average from similar launched items
    - a foundation model zero-shot, as a prior
    - a hand-set ramp curve from the last N launches

STRUCTURAL BREAKS AND CHANGEPOINTS
  A permanent shift in the process: a relaunch, a pricing change, a
  large customer signing or leaving, a pandemic. A model that assumes
  stationarity will average across the break and be wrong on both
  sides.
    - detect: PELT, Bayesian online changepoint detection, Prophet's
      automatic changepoints, or just plotting the series
    - handle: truncate training to post-break, add a level-shift
      regressor, or shorten the training window
    - decide deliberately whether a shift is PERMANENT (level change,
      keep it) or a PULSE (one-off, flag it as an event so the trend
      does not absorb it)

OUTLIERS
  Distinguish two kinds and treat them differently:
    - a measurement artefact or a record of your own downtime: mask it,
      do not let the model learn that mid-March is quiet
    - a real but exceptional event: keep it and model it with an event
      flag, so the pattern is available next time it recurs

CENSORED DEMAND
  You observe SALES, not demand. If you stocked out, or were capacity
  constrained, the history records what you could serve, not what was
  wanted. Train on it naively and you under-build forever, and the
  metric looks fine every time because you predicted your own ceiling
  correctly. Flag constrained periods; exclude or correct them.

THE SELF-INFLUENCING FORECAST
  The forecast changes the outcome it predicts. A high demand forecast
  earns a product more promotion, which makes the forecast come true. A
  churn model that triggers a save-offer destroys its own accuracy when
  it works. Accuracy is then the wrong success metric; measure the
  decision, not the prediction.

MISSING AND IRREGULAR DATA
  Gaps, irregular sampling, timezone and daylight-saving shifts,
  leap years, weeks that do not align with months. Decide explicitly
  whether a missing value is a zero or an unknown - they are completely
  different and confusing them is common.


#######################################################################
PART 14 - PRODUCTION
#######################################################################

A forecasting system is a batch pipeline. Almost nothing here is a
latency problem, and the design should look boring.

    source data -> settled partitions -> feature build (calendar table,
    covariate tables) -> scheduled retrain -> forecast job (all
    horizons, all quantiles) -> forecast table -> consumers

  RETRAIN CADENCE. Schedule-based (weekly is common), drift-triggered,
  or performance-triggered. Note that label delay caps how fast you can
  possibly retrain.
  VINTAGING. Write every forecast with the origin it was made from and
  the model version that made it. You cannot evaluate, debug or defend
  what you overwrote, and it cannot be retrofitted.
  HUMAN OVERRIDE. For large irreversible commitments the system
  recommends and a named person approves. Log every override with its
  reason - overrides are labelled data about what the model is missing.
  FALLBACK. If the job fails, serve the previous run, then seasonal
  naive. A capacity or ordering system cannot accept a null.
  MONITORING:
    rolling accuracy against the backtest expectation
    realised coverage of each published quantile
    input freshness and completeness guards before the job runs
    calendar table health - an alert if a forecast date is near a
      known festival with no calendar entry
    any ratio you converted through, monitored as its own series


#######################################################################
PART 15 - FAILURE MODES TO HAVE READY
#######################################################################

  RANDOM SPLIT              trained on the future, unreproducible score
  LAG SHORTER THAN HORIZON  beautiful backtest, useless in production
  NO BASELINE               nobody can tell whether the model is good
  MAPE AS HEADLINE          systematically biases the forecast low
  POINT FORECAST FOR AN ASYMMETRIC DECISION
  RECURSIVE OVER A LONG HORIZON      compounding error
  UNSETTLED RECENT DATA     a phantom downtrend at the end of every
                            series, learned as real
  OUTAGE TREATED AS DEMAND  the model learns your downtime
  CALENDAR ROT              moving holidays with an unmaintained table;
                            breaks the same month every year
  RATIO DRIFT               forecast the proxy, convert through a ratio
                            that has moved, be wrong invisibly
  CENSORED HISTORY          forecast your own ceiling forever
  OVER-GRANULAR             forecasting per SKU per hour when the
                            decision is monthly and national
  NO VINTAGING              cannot answer "what did we say in March"
  INTERVALS THAT ARE TOO NARROW      acted on, and wrong

  - "Beat seasonal naive or do not ship" is the same discipline as
    "beat BM25" in search/ and "beat the popularity baseline" in
    recommendation/.
  - Censored demand here is the same shape as the popularity feedback
    loop in recommendation/ and position bias in search/: the system's
    own past behaviour limits what the data can teach it.
  - Decision cost as the headline metric is the antidote to the
    Goodhart failure that runs through the RCA repo.
