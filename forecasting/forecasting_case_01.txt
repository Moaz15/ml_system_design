CASE 01: Listening Hours for Capacity Planning
==============================================
CONTEXT
-----------
You are at a subscription audio-content app operating in India. Long-form
spoken content: audiobooks, serialised fiction, self-help, and regional
language originals. Around 150,000 episodes in the catalog, with 40 to 60
new episodes published every week.

SCALE (last month)

  - 8.0M monthly active users
  - 1.2M daily active users on a weekday, about 1.55M on a weekend
  - 45 minutes listened per daily active user on average
  - about 900,000 listening hours per day, about 27M per month
  - streamed on an adaptive ladder averaging roughly 96 kbps, which works
    out to about 1,150 TB of CDN egress per month
  - peak concurrency (people listening at the same moment) around
    140,000, between 20:30 and 22:30 IST
  - peak egress around 13 Gbps
  - three years of daily history at user, session and episode grain


THE FOUR DECISIONS THIS FORECAST DRIVES

  1. CDN COMMITMENT
     A monthly committed egress volume, declared 30 days ahead and locked
     for the month. Committed rate Rs 1.60/GB. Overage is billed at 2.2x,
     so Rs 3.52/GB. Unused commitment is not refunded and does not roll
     over. The line runs about Rs 18.4 lakh a month, Rs 2.2 crore a year.

     Over the last twelve months: overage was charged in four months,
     totalling Rs 21 lakh. In the other eight months, unused commitment
     wasted about Rs 14 lakh.

  2. PEAK STREAMING CAPACITY
     The servers that stream the audio add themselves automatically as
     more people listen at once - but only up to a ceiling. The machines
     are booked from the cloud vendor a month in advance to get a
     discount, and that booking IS the ceiling: the system cannot scale
     past machines we did not book. The booked pool costs about Rs 45
     lakh a year, and the ceiling is set once a month.

     If listeners go past the ceiling, we can add unbooked machines at
     about 3x the price - but they take about 20 minutes to start up and
     begin serving. For those 20 minutes there is nothing to serve the
     extra listeners with, so playback fails for some of them.

     That is what happened on 12 October last year. The number of people
     listening at the same moment stayed above the ceiling for 90
     minutes. Playback failures went from a normal 0.2% of attempts to
     3.4%. About 80,000 people hit at least one failure, and a week
     later that group was about 4 percentage points less likely to still
     be using the app than a comparable group who listened normally that
     same evening.

     So: ceiling booked and not used is wasted at the reserved rate, and
     ceiling booked too low means failed playback for at least the 20
     minutes it takes to add machines.

  3. TRANSCODE AND INGEST
     A reserved GPU pool for ingest, transcode and the bitrate ladder,
     booked quarterly, about Rs 60 lakh a year. Spot capacity exists but
     is not guaranteed during peak hours.

  4. THE CONTENT RELEASE CALENDAR
     The programming team slots its biggest originals into the weeks it
     expects the most listening, and wants 90 days of notice to move a
     release date.


WHAT EXISTS TODAY

  A spreadsheet. Last month's total, multiplied by a growth factor that
  finance picks each quarter, plus a flat 15% "for safety" on anything
  capacity related. One number per month. No range, no interval, no
  stated assumptions. The same single number feeds all four decisions.


WHAT THE DATA ACTUALLY LOOKS LIKE

  HOW THE NUMBERS REACH US

  - The app sends an event every time something happens in a listening
    session - play, pause, seek, finish. Those events flow continuously
    into the data warehouse, which files them one calendar day at a
    time. Each day's file is called a partition.
  - Phones go offline. When that happens the app holds its events and
    sends them once the connection is back, so events belonging to a
    date keep arriving after that date has ended. About 2% arrive late,
    most of them within 6 hours.
  - Because of that, a day's totals keep being corrected for about 48
    hours after the day ends. The number you read this morning for
    yesterday is not the number it will settle at.
  - CDN egress - the bytes actually shipped out to listeners' phones -
    is tracked separately, daily, by the CDN vendor in their own logs.
    That is the number the invoice is computed from, so that is the
    number that costs money.

  THE SHAPE OF THE SERIES

  - Strong weekly cycle. Weekends are materially higher than midweek.
  - Spikes around major festivals and long weekends, and a sustained
    lift through the monsoon months.
  - Diwali last year ran about 35% above the surrounding weeks, for
    about ten days. Diwali falls on a different date each year, and it
    appears only three times in the whole history.

  ONE-OFF EVENTS SITTING IN THE HISTORY

  - 14 March last year: the origin servers were down for 11 hours. The
    origin is the master copy of the audio that the CDN pulls from when
    it does not already have a file cached, so with the origin down a
    large share of playback simply failed. That day's listening came in
    around 60% of a normal Thursday.
  - August last year: a hit original launched - an original being a show
    we produce ourselves rather than license. Listening across the whole
    platform, not just on that show, ran about 12% above where the
    growth line would have put it, for roughly three weeks. It then
    settled at about 4% above.

  CHANGES MADE UNDERNEATH THE SERIES

  - 19 months ago the warehouse changed what counts as a "listening
    hour": sessions shorter than 30 seconds stopped being counted, where
    a session is one continuous stretch of listening. Nobody measured
    what that did to the numbers, so the history before that date and
    the history after it may not be measuring the same thing.
  - 8 months ago a higher-quality 160 kbps tier was added for premium
    subscribers. Bitrate is how much data one second of audio uses, so a
    higher tier ships more bytes for the same minute listened. Average
    bytes per listening hour has been drifting up ever since.

  WHAT DRIVES GROWTH

  - New users come mostly from paid marketing. The marketing calendar is
    planned a quarter ahead, but planned spend and actual spend do not
    always match - campaigns get moved, cut or topped up mid-quarter.


WHY THIS IS ON YOUR DESK NOW

Finance has asked why CDN overage was charged in four of the last twelve
months. Infrastructure has asked why they paid for committed capacity
they did not use in the other eight. The 12 October incident added a
reliability angle that neither of them had raised before.

The CFO wants a committed volume number for next quarter in three weeks.


#######################################################################
YOUR ROLE
#######################################################################

You are a senior ML engineer on the platform data team. You own this end
to end: the data, the model, the service that publishes the number, and
the number that infrastructure, finance and programming actually plan
against. There is one of you and one data engineer, for a quarter.


#######################################################################
QUESTION
#######################################################################

Design the forecasting system. How do you frame the problem, what do you
build, how do you know it is any good, how does it ship and get
monitored, and what do you have ready in three weeks?




#######################################################################
PART A - THE GENERAL PLAYBOOK (use this for EVERY forecasting case)
#######################################################################

Every forecasting design is the same eight questions in the same order.
As with RCA, the discipline is in the ORDER. The classic failure is
reaching question 6 - which model - before answering question 1, and
then building an excellent forecast of a quantity nobody decides on.

   1. WHAT DECISION?   What action changes because of this number?
   2. WHAT TARGET?     Exact quantity, grain, horizon, point or range.
   3. WHAT SCORE?      A loss that matches the decision's cost, and a
                       baseline you are required to beat.
   4. WHAT SHAPE?      Refresh cadence, hierarchy, direct vs recursive.
   5. WHAT INPUT?      Only features knowable at forecast time.
   6. WHAT MODEL?      Climb the ladder. Stop as soon as you are winning.
   7. HOW SHIP?        Rolling-origin backtest, service, human override.
   8. HOW ROT?         Calendar rot, definition drift, self-suppression.

Compressed:  Decision? -> Target? -> Score? -> Shape? -> Input? ->
             Model? -> Ship? -> Rot?


THE FIVE LAWS (these are not negotiable, and they are where most
candidates lose the round)

  L1. NO RANDOM SPLITS. Ever. A random train/test split on a time series
      trains on the future and reports a score you can never reproduce
      in production.

  L2. BEAT SEASONAL NAIVE OR DO NOT SHIP. y_t = y_{t-7} is free, has no
      dependencies and cannot break. Any model that does not beat it by
      a margin worth its maintenance cost is a liability.

  L3. IF THE COST IS ASYMMETRIC, THE ANSWER IS A QUANTILE, NOT A MEAN.
      Almost every real capacity, inventory or staffing decision is
      asymmetric. The mean is the right answer only when the two error
      directions cost the same, which is almost never.

  L4. EVERY FEATURE MUST BE KNOWABLE AT FORECAST TIME, FOR THE WHOLE
      HORIZON. A feature you will not have on the day you forecast is
      leakage, no matter how predictive it looks in the backtest.

  L5. BACKTEST THE WAY YOU WILL FORECAST. Same origin spacing, same
      horizon, same information cutoff as production. Anything else
      measures a system you are not going to run.


STEP 1 - WHAT DECISION?
-----------------------
A forecast is never the product. The decision is the product. Get, for
each consumer of the number:

  - the action they take (commit, provision, hire, schedule, order)
  - the lead time, which fixes the horizon - not your preference
  - the cadence, which fixes how often you refresh
  - the cost of being too low, and the cost of being too high, in money

If two consumers have different lead times or different cost shapes, you
have two forecasting products, however much they share a pipeline.

If no action changes, do not build it. A dashboard is not a decision.


STEP 2 - WHAT TARGET?
---------------------
Pin down four things and say them out loud:

  - THE EXACT QUANTITY. Prefer the quantity the decision is denominated
    in. If the invoice is in bytes, forecast bytes, not a proxy that
    converts to bytes through a ratio that can drift.
  - GRAIN. Daily, weekly, monthly. Forecast at the grain with the most
    structure and aggregate upward. Monthly models over three years of
    history have 36 observations, which is not a dataset.
  - HORIZON. From the lead time, not from habit. The horizon decides
    whether lag features are usable at all.
  - POINT OR DISTRIBUTION. See L3.

Then consider DECOMPOSING the target into factors with different drivers:

      quantity = volume x intensity

  Each factor gets its own model, and the product is the forecast. This
  is worth doing whenever one factor carries the seasonality and the
  other carries a slow structural trend, because it localises error and
  it survives product changes that only move one factor.


STEP 3 - WHAT SCORE?
--------------------
Three tiers, and say all three:

  - POINT ACCURACY: MASE by preference - it is scale free and it is
    literally "how many times better than seasonal naive am I", which
    is the question. Avoid MAPE: it is asymmetric, it punishes
    over-forecast less than under-forecast, and it explodes near zero.
  - DISTRIBUTIONAL: pinball loss at the quantiles you actually use, plus
    coverage calibration - does the P90 contain the truth 90% of the
    time, measured over the backtest.
  - DECISION COST: simulate the real decision over the backtest and
    price it in money. "This saves Rs X a year against the spreadsheet"
    is the number to present. It is the only metric the business can
    argue with, and it is the only one that can tell you when a more
    accurate model is not worth shipping.

Always report the baseline's decision cost alongside yours.


STEP 4 - WHAT SHAPE?
--------------------
  - Batch, almost always. Forecasting is rarely a latency problem.
  - Refresh cadence matched to the decision, plus a daily refresh for
    monitoring even when the decision is monthly.
  - DIRECT vs RECURSIVE multi-step. Recursive feeds its own predictions
    back as lags and compounds error; it is fine for short horizons and
    bad for long ones. Direct trains a separate model per horizon (or
    one model with horizon as a feature) and is the safer default past
    about two seasonal cycles.
  - HIERARCHY. If the number is consumed at several levels, the levels
    must reconcile. Decide bottom-up, top-down or an optimal
    reconciliation, and say why.
  - VINTAGING. Store every forecast with the origin date it was made
    from. You cannot evaluate, debug or defend what you did not store.


STEP 5 - WHAT INPUT?
--------------------
Split features by whether the future value is KNOWN:

  - KNOWN FUTURE (safe at any horizon): calendar, day of week, holiday
    and festival tables, planned promotions, published schedules,
    contracted events.
  - OBSERVED PAST ONLY (safe only at short horizons): lags, rolling
    statistics, recent levels.
  - PLANNED BUT UNCERTAIN: budgets, launch plans. Usable, but measure
    historically how far plan drifts from actual before trusting it.

Time-series leakage is specific and easy to commit: any feature computed
from data that would not have existed at the origin. Recomputing a
rolling mean over the full series before splitting is the classic.

Holiday and festival features need LEAD AND LAG WINDOWS, not a single
day flag, because behaviour ramps before and decays after.


STEP 6 - WHAT MODEL?
--------------------
Climb, and stop as soon as you are winning by enough to justify the
maintenance:

      0. naive                          reference only
      1. seasonal naive                 the baseline you must beat
      2. seasonal naive + drift, or STL decomposition + drift
      3. ETS / Theta                    strong, cheap, surprisingly hard
                                        to beat on clean seasonal data
      4. SARIMAX or Prophet             when exogenous regressors matter
      5. GBDT on calendar + exogenous   quantiles come free via pinball
         (+ lags at short horizons)     loss; the usual practical winner
      6. global deep models             DeepAR, TFT, N-BEATS

Level 6 needs either many related series or a very long history. A
single series with a thousand daily observations does not have the data
to justify it, and saying so plainly is a strength, not a gap.

State for each candidate: training cost, inference cost, how it handles
the seasonality you actually have, how it degrades, and who debugs it at
3am.


STEP 7 - HOW SHIP?
------------------
  - ROLLING-ORIGIN BACKTEST with origins spaced to match the real
    decision cadence, and the full horizon evaluated at each origin.
  - Compare against the incumbent - including the human spreadsheet,
    which is a model and should be scored like one.
  - Human sign-off on high-stakes irreversible commitments. Log every
    override; overrides are labelled data about what the model misses.
  - A fallback that always returns a number. A capacity system cannot
    accept a null.

STEP 8 - HOW ROT?
-----------------
  - CALENDAR ROT: moving festivals need a maintained table. If nobody
    owns it, the model silently breaks the same month every year.
  - DEFINITION DRIFT: someone changes what the metric counts, upstream,
    without telling you. Monitor the level, not just the error.
  - RATIO DRIFT: if you converted through a ratio, monitor the ratio
    separately with its own alarm.
  - SELF-SUPPRESSION: under-provisioning suppresses the demand you then
    record, and next cycle you forecast the suppressed number. Flag
    constrained periods and correct or exclude them.
  - CALIBRATION DECAY: intervals widen or narrow as the regime changes.
    Track realised coverage continuously.


TIMEBOXING A 45-MINUTE ROUND
----------------------------
   ~5 min  clarify - decision, lead time, cost of each error direction
   ~5 min  frame - how many distinct forecasting products is this really
   ~5 min  target definition and decomposition
   ~5 min  the quantile, from the cost ratio
   ~5 min  data hygiene and features
   ~7 min  model ladder and why you stop where you stop
   ~7 min  backtest design and the decision-cost metric
   ~6 min  shipping, monitoring, phasing


COMMON TRAPS
------------
  - Forecasting a quantity nobody decides on.
  - Reporting a point forecast for an asymmetric decision.
  - Random splits, or a single holdout at the end of the series.
  - MAPE as the headline metric.
  - Never quoting a naive baseline, so no one can tell if it is good.
  - Recursive lags over a 90-day horizon.
  - A feature that will not exist at forecast time.
  - Deep learning on one short series.
  - Forecasting at a finer grain than any decision needs.
  - Treating an outage day as demand.
  - Shipping without storing forecast vintages.

0. HOW I AM GOING TO STRUCTURE THIS
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Let me say where I am going, because the framing carries most of the
value in this one.

I will start with the decisions rather than the series, because I think
what looks like one forecasting problem is actually four different
products with four different horizons and four different cost shapes -

what is on the CFO's desk in three weeks.

The headline I want to flag up front: the money here is not in model
accuracy. It is in the fact that today we produce a single number with
no uncertainty attached, and then make a commitment decision with a 2.2x
asymmetric penalty against it. You can capture most of the available
savings with seasonal naive plus an empirical error distribution and a
newsvendor calculation, and zero machine learning. I want to earn that
claim rather than assert it, but it shapes how I would sequence the
work: measurement harness first, quantiles second, model third.

1. CLARIFYING QUESTIONS (six, and two of them change the answer)
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. Is the CDN commitment on TOTAL MONTHLY VOLUME, or on a peak
    transfer RATE? This is the most important question I have. A volume
    commit means I forecast the monthly sum of bytes, and day-to-day
    variance largely cancels within the month. A rate commit means I
    forecast the peak, and the variance is the entire problem. The
    context says committed egress volume, so I will assume volume, but
    if it is a 95th-percentile rate commit - which many CDN contracts
    are - my target changes completely and most of this answer changes
    with it.

Q2. What exactly is "peak concurrent streams", and what is the pool
    ceiling denominated in? The daily maximum of per-minute concurrency
    is a different series from the p99 of per-minute concurrency, and
    the second is far more stable. If the ceiling is in instances or in
    Gbps rather than streams, I would rather forecast the thing the
    ceiling is set in.

Q3. What is the cost of a playback failure? I can price the CDN side
    from the contract, but the capacity side needs a number for what the
    12 October incident actually cost. I would want to convert that
    4-point retention gap into money, because that ratio is what sets
    the safety margin, and right now the 15% in the spreadsheet is
    somebody's guess.

Q4. Is the reserved pool ceiling a hard cap, or can we burst? The
    context says we can burst at 3x with a 20-minute provisioning lag.
    That matters enormously: it means under-provisioning is not a cliff,
    it is an expensive slope with a 20-minute hole in it. So the real
    question is whether we can predict 20 minutes ahead intraday, which
    is a different and much easier problem than predicting a month
    ahead. I will come back to this.

Q5. The listening-hour definition changed 19 months ago and the bitrate
    ladder changed 8 months ago. Has anyone quantified either? If not I
    will, and the first one may cost me a third of my usable history.

Q6. How firm is the marketing plan? The context says planned and actual
    spend diverge. I want the historical distribution of that divergence
    before I put planned spend in a model, because a noisy regressor
    with a confident coefficient is worse than no regressor.


--------------------------------------------------------------------
2. BEFORE I DESIGN ANYTHING: IS THIS WORTH BUILDING
--------------------------------------------------------------------

I want to size the prize first, because it determines how much system
is appropriate, and the honest answer here constrains the design.

    CDN overage, last 12 months                        Rs 21 lakh
    Unused CDN commitment, last 12 months              Rs 14 lakh
    ------------------------------------------------------------
    Identifiable annual waste on the CDN line          Rs 35 lakh

Against a CDN line of about Rs 2.2 crore, that is roughly 16% waste. A
good forecast plus a correctly chosen commit level will not recover all
of it - some of that variance is genuinely unpredictable - but
recovering half is realistic, so call it Rs 15 to 20 lakh a year.

Then the capacity side. One 90-minute incident hit about 80,000 users
with a 4-point retention gap. If a retained user is worth on the order
of Rs 400 in contribution terms - and I would want that number from
finance rather than from me - that single incident cost:

    80,000 x 4% x Rs 400  =  about Rs 12.8 lakh

Which is comparable to a full year of CDN waste, from ninety minutes.
Meanwhile 15% of extra headroom on a Rs 45 lakh pool is about Rs 7 lakh
a year. So on those numbers, carrying generous headroom on capacity is
cheap insurance and we should not be trying to trim it - we should be
trying to never breach it again.

That gives me the shape of the whole engagement: the CDN problem is a
COST OPTIMISATION where I want the commit near the middle of the
distribution, and the capacity problem is a RELIABILITY problem where I
want to sit far out in the tail. Same demand, opposite postures. If we
forecast one number and use it for both, we will always be wrong on at
least one of them - which is precisely what has been happening.

Total recoverable, maybe Rs 20 to 30 lakh a year plus incident
avoidance. That justifies one engineer for a quarter and a small, boring
system with good tests. It does not justify a forecasting platform, a
feature store, or anything with a transformer in it. I would say that
out loud to the stakeholders, because scoping down is cheaper to do in
week one than in month six.


--------------------------------------------------------------------
3. THIS IS FOUR DECISIONS, NOT ONE FORECAST
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Here is how I would separate them, and this table is the main thing I
would put on the whiteboard.

  DECISION          TARGET SERIES        GRAIN  HORIZON  POSTURE
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------
  CDN commit        monthly egress GB    daily  30-60d   near median
                                         -> sum
  Peak capacity     daily peak           daily  30-60d   far tail
                    concurrency
  Transcode pool    hours of audio       weekly 90d      not a forecast
                    ingested                             (see below)
  Release calendar  weekly listening     weekly 90d      ranking only
                    hours

Three observations, and the third is the one I care about most.

FIRST, the horizons are similar but the POSTURES are opposite. CDN wants
roughly the median because overage is only 2.2x. Capacity wants roughly
the 99th percentile because the downside is retention. Those are both
served by one distributional forecast and two different read-offs - so
one model, two quantiles, not two models.

SECOND, the release calendar does not need the level at all. Programming
is choosing WHICH week to launch in. If my forecast is 10% low on every
week but gets the ordering of weeks right, that decision is perfectly
served. So the release calendar is evaluated on rank correlation across
weeks, not on absolute error. Same forecast, completely different
success metric, because it is a different decision.

THIRD, and I would push back here: transcode capacity is not a demand
forecasting problem at all. It scales with hours of audio INGESTED, and
ingest is driven by the publishing schedule, which the content team
owns and already knows 90 days out. That is a join against the content
calendar plus a per-minute transcode cost, not a model. I would kill
that sub-project in the first week and hand programming a spreadsheet
formula. If we later find that ingest volume is itself unpredictable
because creators deliver late, then it becomes a forecasting problem -
but about DELIVERY, not about listening.

Killing one of four is not scope avoidance. It is the highest-leverage
thing I do all quarter, because it stops us modelling a quantity that is
already known.


--------------------------------------------------------------------
4. THE TARGET: THE OBVIOUS ONE IS WRONG
--------------------------------------------------------------------

The title of this case says "listening hours". I do not think listening
hours is the right target for the CDN decision, and this is where I
would spend my time.

The invoice is denominated in BYTES. Listening hours convert to bytes
through a ratio - average bytes per listening hour - and we know from
the context that this ratio has been drifting up for eight months
because a 160 kbps premium tier was added. So a perfect listening-hours
forecast multiplied by a stale ratio gives a wrong bill, and the error
will be systematically low and growing.

Two candidate fixes:

  (a) Forecast daily egress GB directly from CDN logs. It is the billed
      quantity, we have about 1,100 daily observations of it, and the
      bitrate drift is inside the series so the model can absorb it.

  (b) Decompose:   egress_t = listening_hours_t x bytes_per_hour_t
      and forecast the two factors separately.

I would go with (b), and this is a considered choice rather than a
reflex. The two factors have completely different dynamics. Listening
hours carry all the seasonality - the weekly cycle, the festivals, the
monsoon lift. Bytes per hour is a slow structural trend driven by the
premium subscriber mix and the bitrate ladder, with no weekly pattern at
all. Forcing one model to represent both means the model has to learn a
slow trend and a strong seasonal in the same parameters.

Decomposing buys three things. Error is diagnosable - when the bill is
off, I know immediately whether it was demand or mix. A product decision
to change the ladder touches exactly one factor and I can override it
directly instead of waiting for the model to relearn. And the listening
hours factor is reusable for the release calendar and the capacity
model, so I build one demand model and three read-offs.

The risk of decomposing is that errors in the two factors can compound
and the combined interval is not simply the product of the two
intervals. I would handle that by simulating: draw from both factor
distributions jointly in the backtest to get the egress distribution,
rather than multiplying quantiles, which is wrong.

For PEAK CAPACITY the target is daily maximum concurrency, modelled
directly rather than derived from daily hours. Peak depends on the
intraday shape, and the shape shifts - weekends flatten out, festivals
shift later into the night. But I would monitor the ratio of peak to
daily hours as a stability check, because if that ratio moves, the
intraday shape is changing and both models need attention.

One thing I would NOT do: forecast per user, per episode, per language
or per city. Nobody makes a decision at those grains. Aggregate series
are dramatically easier and more accurate, and granularity without a
decision behind it is cost with no return.

On grain and horizon: I forecast DAILY and sum to the month, never
monthly directly. Three years gives 36 monthly observations, which is
not enough to learn anything, while daily gives about 1,100 and carries
the weekly and holiday structure that actually drives the sum.


--------------------------------------------------------------------
5. PICKING THE QUANTILE - THIS IS WHERE THE MONEY IS
--------------------------------------------------------------------

This is the part I would not let get skipped, because it is worth more
than every modelling decision in this answer combined.

We commit C gigabytes at Rs 1.60. We pay for C whether we use it or not.
If actual usage A exceeds C, the excess is billed at Rs 3.52.

    E[cost](C) = 1.60 C + 3.52 E[max(A - C, 0)]

Differentiate and set to zero:

    1.60 - 3.52 P(A > C) = 0
    P(A > C) = 1.60 / 3.52 = 0.4545
    P(A <= C) = 0.5455

So the optimal commitment is the 55th percentile of the monthly volume
distribution. Equivalently, by the critical fractile: the cost of being
one GB short is the overage premium, Rs 3.52 - Rs 1.60 = Rs 1.92, and
the cost of one wasted committed GB is Rs 1.60, giving
1.92 / (1.92 + 1.60) = 0.545.

I want to draw out why that is interesting. Everyone's instinct with a
penalty attached is to commit high and be safe. The arithmetic says
commit barely above the median, because a 2.2x multiplier is a mild
penalty. The current practice of adding a flat 15% safety margin is not
conservative-and-therefore-fine; it is systematically overpaying, and it
is exactly why eight months had unused commitment. The overage in the
other four months was not the failure - at the optimum we EXPECT to go
over in about 45% of months. Somebody needs to hear that clearly,
because right now overage is being treated as an error to eliminate, and
eliminating it entirely would cost more than it saves.

Now the capacity side, same demand, different economics. Under-provision
costs retention; over-provision costs pool spend. From section 2, one
incident ran around Rs 12.8 lakh against roughly Rs 7 lakh a year for
15% more headroom. Even on conservative assumptions the ratio of
underage to overage cost is 50:1 or worse, which puts the fractile at
50/51, about 0.98. So:

    CDN commit           ->  P55 of monthly egress
    Capacity ceiling     ->  P98 to P99 of daily peak concurrency

Same underlying demand model, quantiles chosen from two different cost
functions. If you take one thing from this design, it is that.

Three caveats I would state rather than hide. The fractile assumes the
cost is linear on both sides; if the contract has minimum-commit
penalties, tiered rates or a true-up, I redo it with the real contract
in front of me. Second, I should sanity check against the empirical
distribution rather than trusting a normal assumption, because monthly
totals of a growing seasonal series are skewed. Third, the capacity
number assumes we cannot burst - given we can burst at 3x with a
20-minute lag, the real answer is a P98 ceiling PLUS a fast intraday
trigger, which I will come to.


--------------------------------------------------------------------
6. IS THE DATA REAL? (I will not forecast a series I have not audited)
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Cheap checks, and I would do all of them in week one before any model.

DEFINITION CHANGE, 19 MONTHS AGO. Sessions under 30 seconds were
excluded. This is the biggest threat to the history. I would measure the
step by reconstructing both definitions from raw session data across the
boundary and computing the ratio. If raw sessions are retained I
backfill the old period onto the new definition and keep all three
years. If they are not, I have about 19 usable months, which is one and
a half Diwalis, and I would say so plainly rather than quietly training
on a series with a step in it. A level shift that nobody flags gets
absorbed into the trend, and then the trend is wrong going forward.

BITRATE LADDER CHANGE, 8 MONTHS AGO. Already handled by decomposing -
it lives entirely in bytes-per-hour. I would plot that factor and check
whether the drift is a ramp as premium mix grows, or a step. A ramp I
can extrapolate cautiously; a step I model as a step.

LATE-ARRIVING EVENTS. About 2% arrive after partition close, with
partitions revised for 48 hours. Two rules. First, the forecast job
never reads a partition less than 48 hours old, or it trains on
systematically under-counted recent days and learns a phantom downtrend
at the end of every series. Second, a guard that refuses to run if the
latest usable partition has under 98% of the event volume of the same
weekday four weeks prior.

THE 14 MARCH OUTAGE. That is not demand, it is a measurement of our own
downtime. I mask it and treat it as missing rather than interpolating it
silently - and I would check what the days either side look like, since
outages usually produce a recovery bump as people come back. If I leave
it in, the model learns that mid-March is quiet.

THE AUGUST HIT SHOW. The context says +12% for three weeks settling to
+4%. That is a pulse plus a small level shift, and they need separate
treatment: the pulse gets an event flag so it does not distort the
weekly seasonal, and the +4% is genuine new baseline that the trend
should keep. If I let a single model absorb all of it as trend, I will
forecast a growth rate we are not actually on.

RECONCILE TO THE INVOICE. Our egress series has to tie out to what the
CDN vendor billed, month by month, for the whole history. If our numbers
and the invoice disagree, the invoice wins and I have a data problem to
fix before I have a modelling problem. I would not present a single
forecast before that reconciliation passes.


--------------------------------------------------------------------
7. FEATURES, AND THE FORECAST-TIME CONSTRAINT
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Every feature has to pass one test: will I have its value, for every day
of the horizon, at the moment I produce the forecast?

KNOWN FUTURE - safe at any horizon:
  - day of week, week of year, month, day of month
  - Indian festival calendar with LEAD AND LAG WINDOWS, not single-day
    flags: Diwali behaviour ramps for days beforehand and decays after.
    Regional festivals matter here because the catalog is
    regional-language; Onam and Pongal will move Malayalam and Tamil
    listening without touching the Hindi catalog.
  - long weekends and bridge days, which are not the same as holidays
  - the cricket calendar. IPL and international fixtures are known
    months ahead and they compete directly for evening attention, which
    is exactly our peak window. In this market I would expect this to be
    one of the stronger peak-concurrency features, and it is free.
  - the content release calendar - known originals launching
  - school and board exam season, for the regional student-heavy segments

PLANNED BUT UNCERTAIN:
  - marketing spend. Known a quarter ahead in plan, but the context says
    plan and actual diverge. Before using it I would regress actual on
    planned historically and look at the spread. If plan explains actual
    well, use it. If not, I would rather use a smoothed recent-actual
    install rate and accept that I cannot anticipate a campaign.

OBSERVED PAST ONLY - and this is the important constraint:
  - lags and rolling statistics are enormously predictive at short
    horizons and nearly useless at 90 days.

That last point drives a structural decision. For a horizon of one to
fourteen days, y_{t-7} and a rolling mean carry most of the signal. At
day 90 there is no recent observation to lag from, and feeding the
model's own predictions back recursively over 90 steps compounds error
badly. So I build two configurations of the same pipeline:

  SHORT HORIZON  (h = 1 to 14)   lags + calendar + exogenous
                                 drives the capacity ceiling and the
                                 intraday burst trigger
  LONG HORIZON   (h = 15 to 120) calendar + exogenous + trend only,
                                 direct multi-horizon, no lags
                                 drives the CDN commit and the release
                                 calendar

Same code, two feature sets, horizon as an explicit parameter. I would
not pretend one model serves both well.


--------------------------------------------------------------------
8. MODEL CHOICE - AND WHERE I STOP CLIMBING
--------------------------------------------------------------------

I climb the ladder and stop as soon as the decision-cost metric stops
improving by more than the maintenance is worth.

  1. Seasonal naive, y_t = y_{t-7}.  The baseline. Free, unbreakable,
     and on a series with a weekly cycle this strong it will be better
     than people expect.
  2. STL decomposition, then seasonal naive plus drift on the
     deseasonalised level, plus hand-set festival multipliers.
  3. ETS or Theta. Cheap, well understood, genuinely hard to beat on
     clean seasonal data - Theta has won forecasting competitions
     against far fancier things.
  4. LightGBM on calendar and exogenous features, trained with PINBALL
     LOSS at each quantile I need. This is my expected landing point.
     It handles the festival interactions and the cricket calendar
     naturally, it gives me quantiles directly rather than through a
     normality assumption on residuals, and it is debuggable.

I would NOT go to DeepAR, TFT or N-BEATS here, and I would say why
rather than leaving it implied. Those models earn their keep on many
related series or very long histories. We have one series with about
1,100 daily observations, of which possibly 570 are on the current
metric definition. There is not enough data to justify the parameters,
and there is nobody on a two-person team to debug it at 3am.

The festival point is worth making explicitly. We have seen Diwali three
times. Any model that claims to have learned the Diwali shape from three
observations is fitting noise. So festivals get explicit, hand-specified
event windows with a pooled multiplier across similar festivals and a
prior pulled toward the overall holiday effect - not a freely learned
per-festival coefficient. I would rather have a slightly biased festival
estimate that I can explain and override than a variance-heavy one that
nobody trusts. This is also why the human override in section 10 is not
optional.

For BYTES PER HOUR, the second factor: this is a slow, smooth,
non-seasonal trend. A simple damped trend or a regression on premium
subscriber share is right, and I would damp it rather than extrapolate
linearly, because mix shifts saturate.


--------------------------------------------------------------------
9. EVALUATION - BACKTEST THE WAY YOU WILL FORECAST
--------------------------------------------------------------------

The backtest has to imitate production exactly.

In production, we declare the CDN commit on the 1st, 30 days ahead, and
we only know data settled to 48 hours prior. So the backtest is: for
each month in the history, stand on the 1st, hide everything after the
information cutoff, forecast the next 30 days, sum, and compare. That
gives me 30-odd origins over three years - not a lot, which is itself
worth stating, because it limits how finely I can distinguish models and
argues against picking a complex one on a small measured margin.

For the capacity model, origins are daily with a 30-day horizon, which
gives far more evaluation points.

Metrics, in the order I would present them:

  1. DECISION COST IN RUPEES. Replay the actual commitment decision over
     the backtest under each candidate and price it with the real
     contract. This is the headline and it is the only number the CFO
     should be shown. Crucially, I score the SPREADSHEET the same way -
     the incumbent is a model and it gets a row in the table. If the
     spreadsheet cost Rs 35 lakh and seasonal-naive-plus-quantiles costs
     Rs 16 lakh and LightGBM costs Rs 14 lakh, the interesting finding
     is that most of the gain came from the quantile, not the model.
  2. PINBALL LOSS at P55 and P99, the quantiles we actually consume.
  3. COVERAGE CALIBRATION. Over the backtest, is our P99 exceeded about
     1% of the time? An uncalibrated interval is worse than no interval
     because it is acted on.
  4. MASE against seasonal naive, as the point-accuracy sanity check.
     I would not lead with it and I would not use MAPE at all.
  5. For the release calendar only: Spearman rank correlation of
     predicted versus actual weekly ordering, over a 90-day horizon.

One statistical caution I would raise unprompted: with about 30 monthly
origins I will be comparing models on a small sample, so I would use a
Diebold-Mariano test or a simple block bootstrap over origins rather
than declaring a winner on a raw mean difference. Otherwise I will pick
a model on noise and then defend it for a year.


--------------------------------------------------------------------
10. ARCHITECTURE AND SHIPPING
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Nothing here is real-time. This is a daily batch job with a monthly
decision hanging off it, and the design should look boring.

    app events                    CDN vendor logs
        |                                |
        v                                v
    streaming pipeline               daily pull
        |                                |
        v                                v
    fct_listening_daily            fct_egress_daily
    (revised for 48h)              (ties to invoice)
        |                                |
        +----------------+---------------+
                         v
                  feature build
       calendar | festival table | cricket fixtures | release plan
       marketing plan | premium subscriber share
                         |
                         v
                 weekly retrain  ---->  model registry (versioned)
                         |
                         v
                 daily forecast job
       h = 1..120, quantiles P05 P50 P55 P90 P99, both factors,
       joint simulation for egress
                         |
                         v
       fct_forecast (origin_date, target_date, horizon, quantile,
                     factor, value, model_version)
                         |
       +-----------+-----+------+--------------+
       v           v            v              v
   CDN commit   autoscaler   programming    finance
   recommender  ceiling      week ranking   variance report
   (monthly,    (monthly +   (weekly)       (monthly)
    human       intraday
    sign-off)   trigger)

Runtime budget: the whole pipeline under 30 minutes, running at 07:00
IST on data settled to 48 hours. There is no latency requirement worth
engineering for.

Three things on that diagram that matter more than the boxes.

FORECAST VINTAGING. Every forecast is written with the origin date it
was made from and the model version that made it. Six months from now
somebody will ask what we predicted on 1 March for April, and if we
overwrote it we cannot answer, cannot evaluate, and cannot defend the
system. This costs nothing to do on day one and is impossible to
retrofit.

HUMAN SIGN-OFF ON THE COMMIT. The CDN commitment is monthly, large and
irreversible. The system recommends, a named human approves, and every
override is logged with a reason. Overrides are not a failure of
automation - they are labelled data about what the model does not know,
and after two quarters the override log tells you exactly what feature
is missing.

THE INTRADAY BURST TRIGGER. This is the piece the monthly forecast
cannot solve. We can burst at 3x with a 20-minute provisioning lag, so
the question that actually prevents the next 12 October is not "what
will peak be next month" but "will we breach the ceiling in the next 20
minutes". That is a short-horizon intraday problem on live concurrency
telemetry, and it is a far easier prediction than a monthly one. I would
build it as a simple rule on the current concurrency curve against the
same weekday's shape, triggering a pre-warm well before the ceiling. The
monthly P99 sets the standing ceiling; the intraday trigger catches the
tail the monthly model was never going to see.


--------------------------------------------------------------------
11. FAILURE MODES AND MONITORING
--------------------------------------------------------------------

What I watch, and what it catches:

  - FESTIVAL CALENDAR ROT. Diwali moves every year. If nobody maintains
    the table, the model breaks the same month annually and the failure
    looks like a modelling problem. Named owner, annual review, and an
    alert if any forecast date falls within 14 days of a known festival
    with no calendar entry.
  - BYTES-PER-HOUR DRIFT. Monitored as its own series with its own
    alarm. A product decision to change the bitrate ladder will break
    the bill forecast while listening hours stay perfect, and without
    this alarm the post-mortem takes a week.
  - DEFINITION DRIFT. Alert on a level shift in listening hours that is
    not matched by a shift in sessions or DAU. That pattern means
    somebody changed a definition upstream.
  - LATE OR PARTIAL DATA. The 98%-of-same-weekday guard, and the job
    fails loudly rather than forecasting on a partial day.
  - CALIBRATION DECAY. Rolling realised coverage of each published
    quantile. If the P99 is being exceeded 5% of days, the intervals are
    too tight and the capacity decision is being made on a fiction.
  - ACCURACY DRIFT. Rolling MASE of live forecasts against the backtest
    MASE; alert on two consecutive weeks of material degradation.
  - SELF-SUPPRESSION. The one that is subtle and specific to this case:
    if we under-provision and playback fails, listening drops, and next
    month we forecast the suppressed number and under-provision again.
    Every period where concurrency was at or above the ceiling gets
    flagged, and those days are either excluded from training or
    corrected upward. This is the small version of what case 05 does at
    scale, and it is the only feedback loop in this design.
  - FALLBACK. If the job fails, serve the previous run's forecast, and
    if that is stale, seasonal naive. The autoscaler cannot accept a
    null. A capacity system that returns nothing is worse than one that
    returns something slightly old.


--------------------------------------------------------------------
12. WHAT IS READY IN THREE WEEKS, AND WHAT COMES AFTER
--------------------------------------------------------------------

The CFO needs a number in three weeks, so the sequencing is forced - and
it lines up with where the value actually is.

WEEK 1 - MEASUREMENT AND AUDIT, NO MODEL
  Reconcile our egress series to twelve months of invoices. Quantify the
  19-month definition change and decide how much history is usable. Mask
  the outage, flag the hit-show pulse. Build the rolling-origin backtest
  harness and score the SPREADSHEET on it - so we can say, with a
  number, what the current process costs. That number is what buys the
  rest of the quarter.

WEEK 2 - BASELINE PLUS QUANTILES
  Seasonal naive with drift and hand-set festival multipliers, plus the
  empirical distribution of its own backtest errors. Apply the
  newsvendor fractile. This gives a defensible P55 commit recommendation
  and a P99 capacity ceiling with no machine learning in it at all. My
  expectation is that this captures most of the recoverable Rs 20 to 30
  lakh, because the failure today is the absence of a distribution, not
  the absence of a model.

WEEK 3 - SHIP THE DECISION, NOT THE MODEL
  Deliver the commit recommendation for next quarter with the interval,
  the assumptions and the backtested decision cost written next to it.
  Ship the daily pipeline, the forecast table with vintaging, and the
  capacity ceiling recommendation. Retire the flat 15% margin and
  replace it with a stated fractile.

MONTH 2
  LightGBM with pinball loss, both horizon configurations, exogenous
  regressors including the cricket calendar. Ship it ONLY if the
  decision-cost backtest beats the week-2 baseline by a margin that
  survives a block bootstrap. If it does not, we keep the baseline and I
  report that as a result rather than shipping complexity to look busy.

MONTH 3
  The intraday burst trigger, which is the thing that actually prevents
  another 12 October. Calibration monitoring. Hand programming the
  weekly ranking view.

NOT IN SCOPE, DELIBERATELY: per-language or per-city forecasts, hourly
granularity, a feature store, and any deep model. None of them have a
decision behind them today. If a decision appears, they become easy to
add, because the pipeline is already the right shape.


--------------------------------------------------------------------
13. THE 60-SECOND VERSION
--------------------------------------------------------------------

This looks like one forecast but it is four decisions, and one of them -
transcode - is not a forecasting problem at all, it is a join against
the publishing calendar. Of the rest, CDN commitment wants a quantile
near the median, because the newsvendor fractile on a 2.2x overage rate
is P55, while capacity wants P98 or higher because a breach costs
retention. Same demand, opposite postures, which is why one number
feeding both has been failing on both.

I would not forecast listening hours for the CDN decision. I would
decompose egress into listening hours times bytes per hour, because the
bitrate ladder changed eight months ago and only the second factor
moved. Daily grain, summed to the month, never monthly directly.

Before any of that I audit the 19-month definition change, which may
cost me a third of my history, and reconcile the series to the invoice.

Model-wise I expect to land on LightGBM with pinball loss on calendar
and exogenous features, with the cricket calendar as a real feature in
this market - but I would ship seasonal naive plus an empirical error
distribution in week two, because the money here is in producing a
distribution at all, not in the model that produces it.

Everything is judged on backtested decision cost in rupees, with the
current spreadsheet scored as the incumbent, on rolling origins that
imitate the real declaration date.
